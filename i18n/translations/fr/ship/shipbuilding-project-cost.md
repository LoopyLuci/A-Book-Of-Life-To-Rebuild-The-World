# Shipbuilding Project and Cost Management

> **Domain:** ship | **Difficulty:** journeyman | **Safety:** green

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

A shipyard runs on estimates, and the difference between the estimate
and the price is where the project is won or lost. The key principle is
to build the cost model from the weight and the labour content of the
things that will actually be made, then to manage the change that will
come, because every variation is paid for in money and in time. A
schedule with no float and a cost model with no contingency are both
promises, and both break in the same month.

## Prerequisites

- Reading a general arrangement drawing and a set of detail drawings
- A breakdown structure for the vessel, from group to system to
  component
- Experience of the yard: labour rates, shop hours, and subcontract
  pricing
- Basic estimating theory and the estimate class definitions
- A working knowledge of the build sequence and of the critical path
- The contract and payment terms, and the rules for a variation

## Materials & Tools

- The specification, the drawings, and the master list of materials
- A spreadsheet for the estimate, the cash flow, and the tracking
- A weight and moment schedule for the lightship
- Labour rate cards, wage records, and the productivity history
- Subcontractor quotations and a schedule of the long-lead items
- A programme in a simple bar chart format, and a dependency list
- A change log, a variation register, and a daily site diary

## Procedure

### Build the estimate

1. Break the vessel into a work breakdown structure and estimate every
   group: hull structure, machinery, outfit, electrical, joinery, paint,
   and the design work and the trials.
2. Estimate the material from the drawings and take off, and add the
   waste: a 70 to 85% nesting yield on the plate and a 10 to 20%
   offcut recovery on the sections.
3. Estimate the labour from the tonnage and the productivity history:
   steel erection runs about 20 to 40 man-hours per tonne on a small
   vessel, and welding takes a third of the total hours.
4. Add the overheads at a stated rate, the profit at a stated rate, and
   a contingency of 5 to 10%, then state the estimate class with the
   number, from order of magnitude at -30/+50% to firm at -5/+10%, and
   price the exclusions in writing.

### Plan the programme

1. List the sequence from design through procurement, keel laying,
   erection, outfitting, painting, trials, and delivery, and mark the
   long-lead items: engines, shafts, propellers, and electronics at 12
   to 26 weeks.
2. Draw the dependency list and find the critical path, add the float
   to the non-critical tasks, and set the milestones: keel laid,
   launched, machinery in, sea trials, and delivery, with a hold point
   and an inspection at each one.

### Manage the cash

1. Convert the estimate into a cash flow by month, and match it to the
   payment milestones: an advance, progress payments against the
   milestones, and a retention of about 10% held for 6 to 12 months.
2. Track the actual cost and the earned value each month, and read the
   cost performance index and the schedule performance index before
   the problem becomes obvious on site.
3. Forecast the cost at completion from the spend to date and the work
   remaining, and act early when that forecast moves.

### Control the change

1. Record every change in a variation register with the reason, the
   date, the initiator, and the effect on the cost and the programme.
2. Price each variation before the work starts and get the order in
   writing; the work that has no agreed price is the work that ends in
   a dispute.
3. Control the design changes through a single point of control, and
   re-issue the drawings so that the shop cannot build to a superseded
   revision.

## Safety Notes

- A programme that leaves no float compresses the work into the worst
  possible weather and the shortest possible nights, which is when the
  yard gets hurt; protect the float and protect the rest rules.
- Hot work, lifting, and working aloft are the daily hazards of a
  yard; permit the work, check the equipment, and keep the fire watch
  in place.
- The information on the drawings is the last line of defence: never
  let an urgent programme excuse a missing drawing or an unapproved
  change.
- Manual handling and noise are constant; use mechanical aids for the
  heavy plate and the long sections, and keep the hearing protection on
  the grinder and the chipper.
- Keep the cost and the commercial record accurate and complete, and
  do not let a price conversation become a substitute for a written
  agreement.
- Treat a safety defect found during a hurry as a stop-work item, and
  record it in the site diary so that the delay is understood.
