# Aseptic Technique and Microbial Culture

> **Domain:** biology | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Aseptic technique keeps unwanted microbes out of cultures that must stay
clean, and keeps unknown microbes contained while you work with them. It is
the foundation under diagnostics, food testing, and every starter culture.
The key principle: sterilise everything that touches the medium, and never
open a vessel near your face.

## Prerequisites

- Training in laboratory safety and in biosafety levels
- A working autoclave, or access to one
- A laminar flow cabinet or a clean bench
- Instruction in handling cultures of unknown identity

## Materials & Tools

- Autoclave with a validated cycle and indicator tape
- Pressure cooker usable as a fallback steriliser
- Laminar flow cabinet or still clean bench
- Heat-resistant gloves, apron, and closed shoes
- Culture vessels with loose or vented lids, and an inoculating loop
- Media: nutrient broth and nutrient agar, plus selective media
- Sterile petri dishes, a 0.1 millilitre pipette, and a colony counter
- Incubator or cabinet held at 30 to 37 degrees C
- 70 percent ethanol, or isopropanol, and a surface disinfectant

## Procedure

### Preparing and Sterilising

1. Clean the bench and wipe it with disinfectant before every session, then
   wrap the instruments and media and label each batch with its contents
   and the date.
2. Sterilise liquids in the autoclave at 121 degrees C for 15 minutes at
   about 103 kilopascals, using a validated liquid cycle; never autoclave a
   sealed container.
3. Sterilise glassware and instruments dry at 160 degrees C for 2 hours, or
   at 121 degrees C for 30 minutes on a wrapped cycle.
4. Open the autoclave only once the pressure gauge reads zero and the
   indicator tape or printout confirms the cycle.
5. Cool sterile medium to 45 to 50 degrees C before pouring plates, then
   set the poured plates down lid-side down.

### Transferring Culture

1. Work in the cabinet with the sash low, flame the loop until it glows,
   and let it cool for 5 to 10 seconds before touching the culture.
2. Open vessels as briefly as possible, keep the cap in your hand, never
   talk over an open plate, and close plates promptly.
3. Keep agar surfaces moist by stacking the plates face to face.

### Isolating and Counting Colonies

1. Streak a loopful across the plate in four overlapping quadrants so that
   single colonies are separated.
2. Incubate the plates upside down at 30 to 37 degrees C and read them
    after 18 to 48 hours.
3. Prepare serial tenfold dilutions, from 10 to the minus 1 up to 10 to
    the minus 7, and plate 0.1 millilitre from two or three dilutions onto
    duplicate plates.
4. Count only the plates that hold between 30 and 300 colonies, average
    the duplicates, then multiply the mean count by the dilution factor and
    by 10 to obtain colony forming units per millilitre.

## Safety Notes

- Cultures of unknown identity may contain pathogens; treat every culture
  as potentially hazardous and handle it at the correct biosafety level
  under a supervisor.
- Sterilising pressure vessels can rupture; never autoclave a sealed
  container and never open the door until the gauge reads zero.
- Flames, hot medium, and melted agar cause burns; keep flammables away
  and let agar cool before handling it.
- Ethanol is flammable and its vapour ignites readily; let it fully
  evaporate before using any flame nearby.
- Broken glass and contaminated sharps cause lacerations; put them in
  puncture-proof containers, never in the general waste.
- Disinfectants and antibiotics can sensitise skin; wear gloves and wash
  after every session.
