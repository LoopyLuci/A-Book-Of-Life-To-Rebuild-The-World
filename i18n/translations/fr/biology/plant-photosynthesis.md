# Plant Physiology and Photosynthesis

> **Domain:** biology | **Difficulty:** novice | **Safety:** green

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Photosynthesis turns light, water, and carbon dioxide into sugar and oxygen,
and every crop yield depends on how well a plant manages that trade.
Understanding the light and dark reactions, the limits set by light, water,
and nutrients, and the meaning of deficiency symptoms turns a field
inspection into a diagnosis. The key principle: whichever factor is
shortest sets the pace of the whole crop.

## Prerequisites

- Plant cell and leaf anatomy basics
- A plot or pot of the crop under study, plus a comparison planting
- A soil test, or at least a pH meter

## Materials & Tools

- Healthy and stressed sample plants for side-by-side comparison
- Soil or growing medium samples and a soil test kit
- A refractometer or Brix meter for sap and juice sugar readings
- Light meter or a simple lux meter for canopy readings
- Hand lens, labels, and a notebook for each sample

## Procedure

### Mapping the Two Stages

1. Split the process into the light reactions, which run in the thylakoid
   membrane and produce ATP and NADPH, and the dark reactions, which build
   sugar in the stroma using that energy.
2. Trace the carbon: six CO2 molecules enter the cycle and leave as one
   glucose, at the cost of about 18 ATP and 12 NADPH per glucose.
3. Note that photorespiration returns carbon to the cycle, and that C4
   plants concentrate CO2 near RuBisCO to cut that loss in hot climates.

### Finding the Limiting Factor

1. Measure the light level at the canopy with a meter; above roughly
   30 000 lux most crops are already at light saturation.
2. Watch the stomata: they close when water is short, and a closed stomaton
   cuts photosynthesis and transpiration together.
3. Measure water use, since a healthy crop transpires about 2 to 5 grams of
   water for every gram of dry matter produced.
4. Watch the leaf temperature; above about 30 to 35 degrees C
   photorespiration rises and photosynthesis falls.
5. Change one factor at a time, whether water, light, or nutrients, and
   record the response each time.

### Reading Nutrient Status

1. Take a soil sample from the root zone and test it, aiming for pH 6.0 to
   6.5 for most garden crops.
2. Compare leaf colour against a deficiency chart; nitrogen shortage shows
    as pale older leaves first, while iron shortage shows as pale young
    leaves with green veins.
3. Use the plant's total demand as a guide: nitrogen at 2 to 3 percent of
    dry mass, phosphorus at 0.2 to 0.5 percent, and potassium at 1 to 3
    percent.
4. Correct a confirmed deficiency with a targeted fertiliser rather than a
    general one, and record the rate applied and the date.

## Safety Notes

- Many fertilisers are harmful if swallowed; wear gloves when handling them
  and store them away from food, feed, and animals.
- Some plants carry toxic sap, latex, or alkaloids; wear gloves when
  cutting or mulching and wash your hands afterwards.
- Never apply fresh manure to edible crops close to harvest, and never burn
  manure or residues that may carry pathogens.
- Keep children, pets, and water sources out of any treated area, and
  follow the label interval on every pesticide.
- Use only mature compost, since unfinished compost carries weed seed and
  human pathogens.
- Wear eye protection when slicing stalks and when handling plants with
  irritating hairs or thorns.
