# PCR and Molecular Diagnostics

> **Domain:** biology | **Difficulty:** journeyman | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

PCR turns a trace of DNA into a visible band within hours, so a field clinic
can identify pathogens, crop pests, or adulterated food without a central
laboratory. The result is only as trustworthy as the controls that ran beside
it. The key principle: keep amplified DNA out of the clean area, or the
test will read your own contamination as a positive result.

## Prerequisites

- Formal training in PCR setup and contamination control
- A separated pre-amplification area and a post-amplification area
- Access to a thermocycler and a gel electrophoresis rig
- Pure water, calibrated pipettes, and filter tips
- A working relationship with a reference laboratory

## Materials & Tools

- Specimen: swab, tissue, sputum, or a concentrated water sample
- DNA extraction kit, or a lysis and alcohol precipitation protocol
- PCR master mix with a hot-start polymerase
- Forward and reverse primers for the target organism
- Positive control DNA and a no-template control
- Thermocycler, heat block, and consumables
- Agarose, running buffer, DNA stain, and a DNA ladder
- UV or blue-light transilluminator, and a microcentrifuge

## Procedure

### Extracting the DNA

1. Pre-aliquot the reagents in the clean area before any sample is opened,
   then collect, label, and keep the specimen cool for same-day processing.
2. Lyse the specimen and remove proteins with a salt or alcohol wash, using
   the volumes given for the kit in use.
3. Transfer the DNA pellet to a clean tube with nuclease-free water, keep it
   on ice, and estimate or measure the concentration.

### Setting Up the Reaction

1. Prepare the master mix in the clean area, for all reactions plus ten
   percent extra, at the volumes the polymerase maker specifies.
2. Add template DNA last, change tips after every tube, and never pipette
   with an open amplified tube anywhere near the bench.
3. Include a positive control with known DNA and a no-template control
   filled with water, close the tubes, and spin them briefly.

### Running the Thermocycler

1. Program 25 to 35 cycles of 94 to 95 degrees C for 30 seconds, 50 to 65
   degrees C for 30 seconds, and 72 degrees C for 30 to 60 seconds per
   kilobase of product.
2. Hold at 72 degrees C for 5 minutes, then hold at 4 degrees C, and record
   the program, the run time, and the operator in the log.

### Reading the Gel

1. Cast a 1.5 to 2 percent agarose gel in running buffer, load it with a
   DNA ladder, and load 5 to 10 microlitres of each reaction with dye.
2. Run the gel at 100 to 120 volts until the dye front is about two thirds
    down the gel.
3. Visualise with a DNA stain under the transilluminator, then photograph
    and score the bands.
4. Report a sample as positive only when a band sits at the expected size
    and both controls behaved correctly.

## Safety Notes

- Ultraviolet light from the transilluminator burns skin and eyes; use a
  shield or a blue-light transilluminator and never look at the source.
- Ethidium bromide and related DNA stains are mutagenic; prefer safer
  blue-light stains, wear gloves, and treat stained gels and buffers as
  chemical waste.
- Opening an amplified tube can aerosolise product and contaminate the
  pipette tip, the bench, and every later reaction.
- Specimens may carry hepatitis, tuberculosis, or other blood-borne
  pathogens; treat every sample as infectious and wear gloves, a coat, and
  eye protection.
- Broken glass and gel-slicing tools cause deep cuts; use a gel scoop and
  put glass in puncture-proof containers.
- Work with sharp blades and forceps under a stable light, and never cut
  towards your hand.
