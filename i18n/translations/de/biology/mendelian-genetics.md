# Mendelian Genetics and Selective Breeding

> **Domain:** biology | **Difficulty:** novice | **Safety:** green

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Inheritance follows predictable rules, so crop and livestock traits can be
bred on purpose instead of by luck. Monohybrid and dihybrid crosses show how
alleles segregate and recombine, and repeated selection turns that knowledge
into a reliable line. The key principle: a trait only becomes a stable line
when it is selected for in every generation and the crosses are recorded.

## Prerequisites

- A basic understanding of cells, chromosomes, and gametes
- A seed tray, pots, and a well-drained growing medium
- Labels and a field notebook for recording every cross
- Isolation bags or a screen cage to keep unwanted pollen out

## Materials & Tools

- Parent seed or planting stock of both lines
- Pots, seed trays, and growing medium
- Hand lens or loupe for examining flowers
- Notebook or ledger for cross and harvest records

## Procedure

### Planning the Cross

1. Choose one trait to work on first, such as seed coat colour or plant
   height, and record the parent phenotypes you can see.
2. Confirm that each parent is homozygous by selfing it for two generations
   and checking that the offspring match the parent.
3. Decide the mating scheme: a monohybrid cross for one trait, or a
   dihybrid cross for two traits tracked at once.
4. Label every pot and seed packet before crossing so the pedigree stays
   traceable.

### Making the Cross

1. Emasculate the female flower by removing the anthers early in the
   morning, before the pollen sheds, and bag it to keep stray pollen out.
2. Collect fresh pollen from the second parent and apply it to the stigma
   the same day.
3. Rebag and label the flower with both parent names and the date.

### Reading the Offspring

1. Grow the F1 seed and score each plant. Expect a uniform generation
   showing the dominant phenotype when the parents differ.
2. Self the F1 plants to produce the F2 seed, then grow the F2 generation
   and count the phenotypes.
3. Expect roughly 3:1 for a single dominant trait, or 9:3:3:1 for two
    independent traits, and test the fit with a chi-square test.
4. Keep only the lines whose segregation fits, then select the F2 plants
    that show the desired trait and self them.

### Fixing the Trait

1. Repeat selfing and selection for four to six generations until the
    offspring are uniform, then archive seed and record the pedigree, the
    selection criteria, and the harvest year.

## Safety Notes

- Never eat, taste, or brew any plant or seed used in a trial; many crop
  relatives and their wild relatives are toxic.
- Wear gloves and wash hands after handling, since seed and plant material
  can carry fungal and viral pathogens.
- Keep trial plots away from food crops so no experimental material reaches
  a harvest.
- Work in a ventilated space so that dry seed and chaff dust are not
  inhaled.
- Keep every label legible, because one mislabelled cross wastes a season
  and corrupts the whole ledger.
