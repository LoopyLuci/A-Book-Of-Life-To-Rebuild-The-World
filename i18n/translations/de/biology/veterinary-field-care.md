# Veterinary Field Care and Triage

> **Domain:** biology | **Difficulty:** journeyman | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

In a field setting there is no clinic and no waiting room, so triage order
and clear records matter more than technique. Recognising the emergencies
that kill within hours, giving basic wound and fracture care, and knowing
when to refer protects the animal and the keeper. The key principle: deal
with what kills first, and refer early rather than late.

## Prerequisites

- Formal training in animal handling and first aid
- A veterinarian who will take field calls and instruct by telephone
- A stocked first-aid kit and a written record of every treatment

## Materials & Tools

- Stretcher or plank, and rope for immobilisation
- Sterile dressings, gauze, cotton wool, and tape
- Antiseptic such as chlorhexidine or povidone iodine, and clean water
- Scissors, forceps, sterile syringes, and 18 to 22 gauge needles
- Suture kit and sterile sutures for wound closure
- Haemostatic gauze, padding, and a pressure bandage
- Thermometer, stethoscope, scale, and weight tape
- Heat support, fluids, and a pain relief product prescribed by a
  veterinarian

## Procedure

### Triage Order

1. Check the airway and breathing first; clear foreign material and
   confirm that the chest moves.
2. Control major bleeding with direct pressure and a pressure pad, and use a
   tourniquet only as a last resort.
3. Assess perfusion by checking gum colour, capillary refill time, and the
   temperature of the extremities.
4. Check the nervous system and the level of consciousness, control pain
   early, and record the time of every step, because that record is what the
   referral veterinarian will rely on.

### Handling Wounds

1. Clip the hair around the wound and clean it with antiseptic and water.
2. Irrigate the wound with at least 5 times its volume of clean fluid so
   that debris is washed out.
3. Decide whether to close the wound: a fresh clean wound may be closed,
   while a punctured, torn, or heavily contaminated wound should be left
   open to drain and treated on veterinary direction.
4. Cover with a sterile non-stick dressing over a warm layer, change the
   dressing at least once a day, and give antibiotics and pain relief only
   at the dose and interval a veterinarian has prescribed for that animal,
   recording every dose, time, and route.

### Stabilising Fractures and Checking Condition

1. Splint with padded splints that span the joint above and below the
   break, and check that the toes stay warm and keep their sensation.
2. Recheck a splinted limb every 4 to 6 hours, since swelling inside a
    tight splint is a common cause of tissue death.
3. Estimate fluid loss from the clinical signs: a skin tent that returns
    in under 1 second is normal, 2 to 3 seconds suggests about 8 percent
    dehydration, and more than 3 seconds points to 10 to 12 percent.
4. Plan the replacement from that estimate, working out the deficit as
    body weight in kilograms times the dehydration percentage times 10 to
    get millilitres, add the maintenance rate the veterinarian sets, and
    refer the case for a deep wound, a major bleed, a suspected internal
    injury, a fracture that cannot be stabilised, a prolapse, or any sign
    of systemic infection.

## Safety Notes

- An animal in pain may bite or kick without warning; muzzle it where safe
  and never work alone with a large animal.
- Never give a drug, a dose, or an injection that a veterinarian has not
  prescribed for that animal; the wrong drug or the wrong dose can kill.
- Do not dig out an embedded object such as a hook or an arrow yourself,
  because removing it may open a bleed that packing around it controls.
- Some animal diseases pass to people, including rabies, ringworm, and
  leptospirosis; wear gloves and a mask, and cover wounds when handling.
- Tetanus enters through any break in the skin; keep the vaccination record
  current and cover cuts on the handler.
- Dispose of carcasses, needles, and blood-soaked dressings by burial away
  from water sources or through a rendering service; never burn or bury a
  carcass near a stream.
- Decontaminate boots, tools, and vehicle tyres between farms, and never
  move a sick animal through a group without veterinary advice.
