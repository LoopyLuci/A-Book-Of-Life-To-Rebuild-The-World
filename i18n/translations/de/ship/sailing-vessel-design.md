# Sailing Vessel Design

> **Domain:** ship | **Difficulty:** journeyman | **Safety:** green

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

A sailing vessel is a system in which the hull's shape, the keel, the rig,
and the ballast all argue about one question: where the wind's force is
applied against where the hull resists it. The key principle is that a
sailboat is not steered by the rudder alone but balanced, and the balance
between the centre of effort of the rig and the centre of lateral
resistance of the hull is what makes the boat go. Get that balance wrong
and no amount of sail area will help.

## Prerequisites

- Naval architecture basics: displacement, hydrostatics, and the righting
  moment curve
- The rig: the sails, the spars, and the vocabulary of a sail plan
- The forces on a sail from the apparent wind
- The metacentric height and the effect of ballast
- Wind data and sea conditions for the intended waters

## Materials & Tools

- The lines plan and the offsets
- The sail plan with the areas and the centres of effort
- The weight and moment schedule
- A righting moment calculation or a stability booklet
- Wind data for the local conditions
- Scales, a drawing board, and a graph of the righting arm
- A ship's weight to weigh the rig components and the gear

## Procedure

### Set the hull's character

1. Choose the displacement-to-length ratio: about 100 to 200 for a modern
   racer, 200 to 400 for a performance cruiser, and 400 to 800 for a heavy
   displacement boat; the number sets how the boat feels and how fast she
   can go.
2. Check the stability: the initial metacentric height must stay positive
   with the ballast in, and the righting moment at 30 to 40 degrees of heel
   must exceed the rig's heeling moment by a clear margin.
3. Size the ballast: 30 to 45 percent of the displacement for a
   performance cruiser, carried in a fin keel, with lead or cast iron
   where the depth of the keel allows it.
4. Shape the hull for the job: a fine entrance and a narrow beam for
   speed, a fuller section and more flare for power in a breeze.

### Set the rig

1. Match the sail area to the displacement: the ratio of square metres of
   sail to tonnes of displacement runs from about 6 to 10 for a cruising
   boat to 15 to 30 for a racer.
2. Put the centre of effort where it can work: at or slightly forward of
   the centre of lateral resistance, typically 3 to 15 percent of the
   waterline length forward, so that the boat is balanced rather than
   permanently weatherly.
3. Size the rig for the wind: the wind gradient gives a speed at the
   masthead of V x (z/10)^0.14, so a 15 m masthead in a 20 knot breeze at
   10 m feels about 21 knots.
4. Decide the reefing early: the working sail area has to be manageable,
   so a boat for stronger winds needs a reefed main and a smaller
   headsail, with the halyards reachable from the cockpit.

### Check the loads

1. Compute the rig's heeling moment at the design wind speed from the
   sail area and the height of the centre of effort above the centre of
   lateral resistance.
2. Apply the design rule that the heeling moment at the design speed
   should be no more than about half of the maximum righting moment, so
   the boat keeps a reserve of stability.
3. Check the rig's structure: the mast's section modulus against the side
   force, and the chainplates and the shrouds against the heeling moment,
   with a spreader or a bent mast if the loads are high.
4. Check the reefed and the gaff cases as well, because that is how the
   boat will actually be sailed in bad weather.

### Fit the boat out for the conditions

1. Choose the rig for the local water: reefing, the number of sails, a
   roller reefing main for a short-handed crew, and a self-tacking jib
   where the crew is small.
2. Provide the tankage: a fixed ballast tank with a hard drain, and
   fresh water and fuel tanks that share no compartment with the sea
   chest.
3. Fit the safety gear and the sailing aids: a lifejacket for every
   person on deck, a life raft, a hand pump, a spare tiller, and an
   anchor with enough rode to hold.
4. Trial the boat: check the balance on a reach with the main alone, then
   with the full sail in a fresh breeze, before the offshore passage.

## Safety Notes

- Heeling is the first warning: a boat that will not come back up past
  30 to 40 degrees is overpowered, and recovering from a capsize is far
  harder than preventing one.
- Lines under load are dangerous; a sheet or a halyard can part and whip
  across the deck, so keep hands off a loaded line and keep the crew
  inboard and clear of the shrouds.
- Never go forward alone and never in heavy weather; a harness is the
  answer when the deck is wet.
- Cold water kills quickly: wear the flotation and keep it on, not under
  the oilskin when it is dry.
- The standing rigging is the boat's structure in disguise; keep it tight,
  the turnbuckles locked, and the chainplates sound.
- Reduce sail early rather than late, and trust the reefing to be usable
  in the dark and in a hurry.
- Carry the knife and know the release for a jammed sheet; a trapped
  crew member on a heeled deck has very little time.
