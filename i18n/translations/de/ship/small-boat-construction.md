# Small Boat Construction

> **Domain:** ship | **Difficulty:** journeyman | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

A small boat is a skeleton, a skin, and a set of joints, and every one of
those joints is a place where the water gets in. The key principle is the
choice of shell: the clinker hull overlaps its planks and makes its own
joints, while the carvel hull lays the planks edge to edge on a frame and
depends on the caulking and the glue. Both work, and the choice is really
about the tools at hand, the wood available, and the tolerance the yard
can hold.

## Prerequisites

- Lofting and the method of lifting
- Choosing the boat type and reading its lines
- Wood selection, seasoning, and the marine plywood grades
- Caulking, fastening, and the tools that go with them
- Basic carpentry and the safe use of hand and power tools

## Materials & Tools

- Marine plywood of 12 to 18 mm for a carvel hull, cedar or mahogany for
  strip planking, and oak or ash for the keel, stem, and sternpost
- Copper rivets of about 4.8 mm with roves, or bronze screws
- Cotton or oakum caulking and a flexible marine sealant
- Boat nails, roves, and clench rings
- Marine varnish or paint over an epoxy primer
- Caulking irons, mallets, clamps, and a caulking bucket
- A drawing board, a scale rule, templates, and a set of chisels
- A planer, a bandsaw, and a drum sander
- A personal flotation device for every person on the boat

## Procedure

### Choose the shell and the shape

1. Choose the construction: clinker for a boat built from strip or from
   sawn boards with a lot of hand work and a forgiving joint, and carvel
   for a plywood or a strip-planked hull that is fair, stiff, and quiet.
2. Set the proportions: a length to beam ratio of about 2.5:1 for a
   stable tender to 4:1 for a fast open boat, with a deadrise of 8 to 20
   degrees.
3. Loft the boat full size on the floor with the stations at every eighth
   of the length, and check the sheer and the plan before cutting
   anything.
4. Make the moulds from the lofted stations and set up the strongback,
   the keel, the stem, and the transom.

### Build the backbone

1. Cut the keel, the stem, and the sternpost from oak or ash, scarf or
   bolt the joints, and bolt them to the moulds.
2. Install the frames from the station templates with the lands in place
   and the frame's cutout faired along the keel.
3. Check the alignment: the stem and the transom must be square to the
   keel and to each other, and the diagonals must agree within about
   3 mm.

### Plank the hull

1. For a clinker hull, bevel the planks and space them at 9 to 19 mm
   thick with a lap of 25 to 38 mm, then fasten each plank through the
   overlap into the frame.
2. For a carvel hull, fit the panels or the strips edge to edge with a
   bevel of about 1:1, then work the joints down with a caulking iron and
   a wooden mallet.
3. Caulk and seal the seams with cotton or oakum driven with a caulking
   iron, and bed the covering in a flexible sealant; leave the outboard
   ends of the seams clear of sealant so that trapped water can drain.
4. Turn the boat over, fair the hull, and sand through 120 and then 180
   grit before coating.

### Fit the boat out

1. Fit the deck and the seats, with a camber of about 1 in 20 so that
   water runs off instead of pooling.
2. Install the flotation; the United States recreational boat rules in
   46 CFR subchapter I require flotation for boats under 20 ft and for
   boats of 20 ft and over that are sold here.
3. Fit the drain and the plug, the bailer or the pump, and the rudder on
   its gudgeons, and check the rudder's clearance in both directions.
4. Launch the boat and fix the leaks in the water before painting; a stiff
   brush and a paste of sawdust and thinner will find the leak for you.

## Safety Notes

- The tools are the hazard: a chisel, a drawknife, and a bandsaw will
  take a hand, so clamp the work and keep the fingers clear of the
  blade.
- Lofting full size takes a large flat floor and a lot of room; do not
  loft in a space where people walk.
- A clinker hull with its frames is heavy to turn over; use the
  strongback and get help.
- Rot is the enemy: probe the keel and the garboard strakes for softness,
  treat the wood with a preservative, and keep the boat drained.
- Marine varnish and solvents are flammable and give off vapour;
  ventilate, and keep them away from any flame.
- The sea takes small boats quickly: fit the flotation, wear it, and carry
  a bailer and a paddle.
- A man overboard in a small boat has minutes, not hours; the standard is
  that the person in charge has three minutes to find and recover anyone
  who falls overboard, so keep a light line and a knife within reach.
