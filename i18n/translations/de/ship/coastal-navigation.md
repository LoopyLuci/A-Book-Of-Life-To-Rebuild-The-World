# Coastal Navigation and Piloting

> **Domain:** ship | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Coastal navigation is the craft of fixing a vessel's position against
charted marks and then holding a course through water that is never
still. The key principle is that a position is only useful if it is
plotted on the chart and acted on: plot every fix, keep the chart and
the tide tables open, and never leave the bridge without a plan for the
leg ahead. Tides, currents, and shoals change the effective chart, and
the chart only tells you where the bottom was.

## Prerequisites

- Ability to read a nautical chart, a tide table, and a light list
- Competence in relative and true bearings, and in the use of a compass
  and a hand-bearing compass
- Basic trigonometry for the triangle of positions and for the tidal
  diamond method
- The Collision Regulations: the International Regulations for
  Preventing Collisions at Sea
- A working knowledge of the local weather and of the tidal streams

## Materials & Tools

- Nautical charts for the area, on paper and on an electronic display
- Dividers, parallel rules, a compass rose template, and a course
  board
- Hand-bearing compass, magnetic compass, and a GPS or radar receiver
- Chart 1 for the area, and the list of lights
- Tide tables, tidal stream tables, and the almanac
- Guide to light characteristics and fog signals, and the Rules of the
  Road
- Weather forecast, a barometer, and a VHF radio
- Lifejacket, an EPIRB, and a log book

## Procedure

### Plan the passage

1. Mark the departure, the waypoints, the hazards, and the abort
   points on the chart, and set the minimum depths, the required
   clearance, and the limiting conditions for the passage.
2. Read the tidal direction for the entrance at the planned time, and
   check the tidal range at springs and at neaps.
3. Set the courses and the distances between the waypoints, and note
   the marks or the bearings that confirm each one.

### Fix the position

1. Take a bearing to a charted object with a hand-bearing compass and
   record the time, the bearing, and the object.
2. Plot the bearing on the chart, and for a single bearing draw a
   relative bearing line and refine it from the estimated position.
3. Take a second and a third bearing from one station, with the
   bearings between 30 and 60 degrees apart, and plot the triangle of
   positions to get the fix, then check it against the dead-reckoning
   position and the time run and treat a disagreement of more than a
   few hundred metres as proof that one of the inputs is wrong.

### Work the tide and the current

1. Apply the tide to the charted depths: the charted depth plus the
   height of tide minus the draft must exceed the required clearance.
2. Keep at least 10% of the draft, and never less than 0.5 m, of water
   under the keel in a narrow channel.
3. Use the tidal diamond method on a chart with diamonds to work out
   the strength and the direction of the stream at a given time, and
   set the course to allow for it.

### Run in reduced visibility

1. Sound the prescribed signals, keep a continuous watch by sight and
   hearing, and reduce speed to the minimum steerage way.
2. Post extra lookouts, one on each side with a line of sight across
   the bow, and keep the radar and the echo sounder running.
3. If the position is uncertain, stop the engines and listen, or
   anchor if the depth and the ground allow it.

## Safety Notes

- Drowning is the real hazard on any coastal passage; wear a lifejacket
  on deck, and treat cold water and a capsize in a confined space as
  immediately life-threatening.
- A grounding on a sandbank can open the hull, so keep a proper
  clearance under the keel and reduce speed when the echo sounder
  shows a shoaling bottom.
- Do not navigate in fog without a chart, a compass, and a method of
  fixing by sound; the most common coastal casualty is a vessel that
  continued at speed with no position.
- Check the tidal window before entering a bar or a restricted channel,
  because an ebb tide on a bar can set a vessel beam-on to the
  breakers.
- Keep a continuous watch and comply with the Collision Regulations; a
  sound signal made in time is worth more than any electronic aid.
- Work at height on a mast or in a cockpit only with appropriate
  training, supervision, and equipment, and stay clipped on in any
  force.
