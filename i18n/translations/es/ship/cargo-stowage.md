# Cargo Handling and Stowage

> **Domain:** ship | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** green

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Cargo decides whether a vessel floats, stays upright, and arrives in
order, and the discipline is to treat weight as a design input rather
than a loading accident. The key principle is that every kilogram has a
position: keep the weight low, centred, and away from the end that
lifts first, secure it so it cannot move, and recompute the
displacement, the trim, and the stability once the load is aboard. A
lashing is never a substitute for a good stowage.

## Prerequisites

- The vessel's deadweight, lightship weight, and weight and moment
  schedule
- The cargo securing manual and the loading plan for the voyage
- The stability booklet and a way of applying the free surface effect
- The dangerous goods segregation table for anything hazardous aboard
- Basic arithmetic with decimal points, and a pocket calculator

## Materials & Tools

- Cargo securing manual, stability booklet, and draft marks
- Lashing straps with a legible maximum safe working load, and a
  ratchet or tensioner
- Dunnage bags, soft chocks, and wedges of timber for blocking and
  shoring
- Turnbuckles, shackles, and deck fittings of a known safe working load
- A scale or a certified set of deck weights for a pallet or a crate
- A cargo plan with the weight and the position of every item marked
- Tarpaulins, absorbent pads, and a spill kit for liquid cargo

## Procedure

### Plan the load

1. List every item with its weight, its dimensions, and its centre of
   gravity, and total them to get the cargo deadweight.
2. Add the fuel, the water, the stores, and the crew to get the total
   deadweight, and check it against the vessel's maximum displacement.
3. Divide the total weight between the longitudinal positions so the
   vessel floats level or with a trim agreed in advance; keep the trim
   within 2% of the length overall, and stack the cargo low with the
   heavy items on the bottom and on the centreline.

### Secure the cargo

1. Calculate the restraining force as the item's weight times an
   acceleration factor of 0.5 g to 1.0 g for heavy items at sea, and
   choose lashing that exceeds it.
2. Work each lashing at no more than 80% of the marked maximum safe
   working load on a ratchet in good condition, and never use a strap
   with cuts, kinks, or damaged stitching.
3. Pass the lashing around the item and down to a deck ring or another
   structural point, pull it until the item no longer moves under hand
   pressure, and block heavy items with dunnage and shoring so that
   they carry the load by bearing and friction rather than by the
   lashings alone.

### Handle dangerous goods

1. Classify each item and check it against the segregation table,
   keeping incompatible classes apart by the stated separation.
2. Mark the packages with the required labels and stow them away from
   the accommodation, from the sources of ignition, and from the food
   and the water.
3. Record the quantity, the class, and the position of every dangerous
   goods item in the cargo plan, and check the exemptions and the
   placard thresholds before loading.

### Check the stability before sailing

1. Recompute the displacement, the trim, and the metacentric height
   with the actual load, the fuel at its working level, and the tanks
   either full or empty, and apply the free surface correction.
2. Walk the deck and confirm that the lashings are tight, that nothing
   bears on a hatch cover, and that the drains and the scuppers are
   clear.
3. Measure the draft at the marks and compare it with the calculated
   draft for the actual water density, then record the figure before
   departure.

## Safety Notes

- A shifting cargo is the most common cause of a capsize; check the
  stowage and the lashings again after any change of position, and
  never sail with a lashing that is not at its working tension.
- Never exceed the vessel's maximum deadweight or the rated stacking
  load; the hatch covers and the deck fittings are rated and the
  cargo is not.
- Stack heavy cargo low and against the hull and keep the deck edge
  clear, because a shed load on a working deck is a hazard to the crew
  and it moves the centre of gravity.
- Dangerous goods can burn, poison, or corrode. Know the class before
  loading, stow the package as the code requires, and carry the
  emergency response information for the cargo aboard.
- Wear gloves, eye protection, and safety footwear when handling
  lashings; a ratchet under tension and a parted strap are both
  dangerous.
- Keep the stability information current and confidential, and do not
  carry passengers or use the vessel beyond its certified limits.
