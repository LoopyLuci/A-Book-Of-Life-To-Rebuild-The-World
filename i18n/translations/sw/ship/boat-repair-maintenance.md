# Boat Repair and Maintenance

> **Domain:** ship | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

A boat is lost slowly, through its seams, its fastenings, its coatings,
and its drains, and almost every loss starts as a small leak that was
never diagnosed properly. The key principle is to find where the water
came from before deciding what to repair, because a patch that does not
address the cause fails in the same way next season. Work from symptom
to cause: soak test, sound the wood, repair the structure, reseal the
seams, then recoat.

## Prerequisites

- Familiarity with the hull, the framing, and the layout of the
  plumbing and the electrical system
- Ability to read a simple scale drawing and to use a hand drill, a
  soldering iron, and a caulking gun
- Access to a slipway, a trailer, or a hoist for hauling the boat out
- A working knowledge of the coatings, fillers, and solvents used on
  the hull

## Materials & Tools

- Marine plywood, hardwood, and hull planking in the correct species and
  thickness
- Bronze screws, sealed fasteners, and matching hardwood plugs
- Epoxy or vinylester resin with hardener, and polyester filler
- Butyl seam tape 6 to 10 mm wide, or oakum with a polyurethane sealant
- Caulking gun, putty knives, a rubber mallet, and a wooden caulking iron
- Balsa and resin for a large core, or glass cloth and epoxy for a small
  split
- Sandpaper in 80, 180, and 220 grit, a belt sander, and a shop vacuum
- Antifouling paint, primer, marine topcoat, and a set of zinc anodes
- A depth sounder, a propeller pitch brush, and a spares box
- Gloves, eye protection, ear protection, and a respirator for sanding

## Procedure

### Survey and diagnose the leak

1. Dry the boat out, then wet it again in one place at a time and watch
   where the water appears on the inside.
2. Soak test the hull in calm water for at least 30 minutes, loaded as it
   would be at sea, and mark the level the water reaches inside.
3. Sound the planking with a mallet or an awl; a dull thud instead of a
   sharp knock means soft wood behind the skin, and the transom,
   garboard, keel, and stem are the first places to check.

### Repair the structure

1. Cut out the damaged wood back to sound material and square the edges
   of the opening so a patch will seat flat.
2. Cut a patch of the same species and thickness, dry fit it, and hold it
   with cleats while the adhesive cures, which takes 24 hours for epoxy.
3. Set the fastener heads below the surface and fill the holes with
   matched plugs, then seal the inside seams with a flexible sealant
   rather than rigid filler so the patch can work with the hull.

### Reseal the seams

1. Open the seam with a caulking iron and a mallet, remove the old
   compound, and dry the wood back to the bare timber.
2. Wind a length of butyl tape into the seam, or pack oakum and apply
   the sealant, then tool the joint within the working time of the
   compound, typically 30 minutes, and let it cure for 48 hours.

### Coat and protect

1. Sand the coating system back with 80 grit, finish with 180 and 220
   grit, and remove every trace of dust with a vacuum and a clean cloth.
2. Apply two coats of primer and two of topcoat, following the wet-on-wet
   and overcoat intervals on the data sheet, then apply the antifouling
   below the designed waterline.

### Lay up for the season

1. Fit the drain plugs, renew the sacrificial anodes, grease the
   propeller shaft and the pintle, and close the seacocks and the fuel
   and water vents.
2. Renew the shore power lead, the bilge pump impeller, and the
   extinguishers, and store the boat on rated stands with the hull
   supported along its length.

## Safety Notes

- Falls from a boat cradle or a slipway kill more people than the
  repairs do; work at height only with appropriate training,
  supervision, and equipment, and wear a harness on a ladder.
- Rot repair exposes you to old coatings and old wood: pre-1980 boat
  paint and primers can contain lead, and some antifouling compounds
  contain organotin or copper compounds. Test suspect coatings, wear
  gloves and a respirator, and never sand indoors without extraction.
- Epoxy and vinylester resins, styrene-based fillers, and polyurethane
  sealants are sensitising and toxic before they cure. Work outdoors or
  with local extraction, wear eye protection, and treat any skin contact
  as an irritant exposure.
- Solvents such as acetone, styrene monomer, and thinner are flammable
  and give off vapour that ignites easily. Keep them away from sparks,
  keep a fire extinguisher at hand, and ventilate the space.
- Sanding and grinding produce fine dust that is both a respiratory
  hazard and a fire load; use a vacuum, a suitable respirator, and
  hearing protection.
- Never work under a hull held up by a jack alone; use rated stands on
  firm ground, and keep hands clear while a boat is being hoisted.
- Disconnect the shore power and the battery before working on the
  electrical system, and use a residual current device on any mains
  supply used near the water.
