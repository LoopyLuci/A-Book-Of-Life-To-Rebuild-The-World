# Enzymes and Fermentation

> **Domain:** biology | **Difficulty:** novice | **Safety:** green

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Enzymes are biological catalysts that make reactions fast enough to be
useful, and the bacteria and yeasts that use them turn sugar into food,
drink, and useful chemicals. Control of temperature, pH, and time decides
which organism wins and what it makes. The key principle: a ferment is a
controlled culture, and an uncontrolled one is a spoilage risk.

## Prerequisites

- A basic understanding of acids, bases, and pH
- Clean glass jars with lids that seal but can vent gas
- A thermometer and pH strips or an indicator

## Materials & Tools

- Sugar source: honey, molasses, fruit, milk, or grain
- Starter culture: baker's yeast, live yoghurt culture, or sourdough
- Salt, and herbs or spices for flavour
- Clean jars, a weight to hold the lids down, and a straining cloth
- Thermometer, pH strips, and a hydrometer for liquid brews

## Procedure

### Understanding Enzyme Behaviour

1. Name the substrate, the enzyme, and the product for the process you
   care about.
2. Measure the rate by following change in pH, temperature, or product
   over time.
3. Raise the temperature by 10 degrees C and expect the rate to roughly
   double, until the enzyme denatures and the rate falls away.
4. Hold the pH within a couple of units of the enzyme optimum, near pH 2
   for pepsin and near pH 8 for trypsin.
5. Stop the reaction by heating the mixture to 70 to 80 degrees C once the
   product is made.

### Running a Starter Culture

1. Dissolve the sugar in water at about 10 to 20 percent by weight, then
   bring the mixture to the starting temperature of the culture: 35 degrees
   C for bread yeast, 42 to 46 degrees C for yoghurt, and 18 to 25 degrees
   C for most lactic cultures.
2. Add the starter at 5 to 20 percent of the volume, stir it in, and hold
   it closed to the air for as long as the culture needs.
3. Allow 4 to 8 hours for yoghurt and 4 to 12 hours for a sourdough rise,
   judging progress by taste and smell rather than by the clock alone.

### Preserving the Product

1. Salt vegetables at 2 percent by weight and hold them under the brine for
   2 to 4 weeks.
2. Check that the brine has dropped below pH 4.0, by titration or pH
    strip, before the jar is stored.
3. Refrigerate the finished food, or keep it sealed in a cool place below
    15 degrees C.
4. Save a portion of the starter as the next inoculum, and label it with
    the date and the crop it was used on.

## Safety Notes

- Low-acid anaerobic foods, including home-canned vegetables and fish, can
  carry Clostridium botulinum spores whose toxin is odourless and tasteless;
  only eat food that was pressure-canned or acidified to pH 4.6 or below.
- Ferments can produce methanol and other higher alcohols; keep distillation
  equipment away from open flames and never ferment in a sealed container.
- A fermenting mash releases carbon dioxide; never seal it airtight, and
  open it away from your face because the gas can cause pressure injury.
- Mould on the surface of a ferment can carry toxins; discard the whole
  batch rather than skimming the top off.
- Wash hands and utensils between batches so that spoilage organisms are
  not carried from one ferment into the next.
- Keep cultures away from children and animals, and never use utensils
  that also handle raw meat.
