# Corrosion and Galvanic Protection

> **Domain:** chemistry | **Difficulty:** journeyman | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Corrosion is the electrochemical return of a metal to a more stable form, and it
eats through tanks, roofs, tools, and hulls faster than most people expect.
Controlling it means choosing compatible metals, isolating dissimilar pairs,
and applying coatings or sacrificial anodes. The key principle is that two
different metals in an electrolyte form a galvanic cell, and the less noble
metal corrodes at the anode.

## Prerequisites

- Basic chemistry and the ability to identify common metals
- Access to the water or soil chemistry at the site
- Safe practice with sharp tools and with abrasive preparation

## Materials & Tools

- Test coupons of mild steel, zinc, aluminium, copper, and 316L stainless
- Salt solution at 3 to 5 percent, plus distilled water
- Steel wool or 180 grit abrasive, degreaser, and clean rags
- Zinc washers or zinc-coated sheet, and zinc or magnesium anode blocks
- Bitumen, tar, or marine paint, plus wax or oil
- Multimeter and, if available, a reference electrode
- Wire brush, masking tape, masking compound, and a depth gauge

## Procedure

### Stage 1: Assess the environment

1. Record the pH, the chloride content, and the dissolved oxygen of the water
   or soil the structure sits in, along with the operating temperature, since
   corrosion rates roughly double for every 10 °C of rise.
2. List every pair of dissimilar metals in contact; each pair is a galvanic
   cell waiting to run.

### Stage 2: Measure the tendency to corrode

1. Clean two coupons of each metal with abrasive, rinse them in distilled
   water, and dry them.
2. Suspend each metal in its own container of the same test solution and
   leave it for a fixed time.
3. Record the mass change and the open-circuit potential against the
   reference electrode; the lower the potential, the more active the metal.

### Stage 3: Break the galvanic couple

1. Separate dissimilar metals with nylon washers, rubber gaskets, or a zinc
   washer placed under the fastener.
2. Coat the joint with a compatible sealant so water cannot reach the
   interface.
3. Match the fastener to the parts it holds wherever possible, such as
   aluminium with aluminium and steel with steel.

### Stage 4: Protect the surface

1. Remove rust and mill scale, then coat with a zinc-rich primer, paint, or a
   bituminous layer.
2. Protect threads and hidden surfaces with zinc washers and a
   corrosion-inhibiting grease.
3. For buried or submerged steel, fit a sacrificial anode of zinc or
   magnesium sized so that it is consumed before the structure.

### Stage 5: Monitor the result

1. Measure pit depth with a depth gauge at fixed intervals, record it on one
   chart, replace sacrificial anodes once more than half the mass is gone,
   and inspect coatings for blistering once a year.

## Safety Notes

- Acids used for pickling and descaling burn skin and eyes. Wear goggles,
  gloves, and an apron, and always add acid to water.
- Rust scale and sharp edges at the site of corrosion cause cuts. Wear gloves
  and eye protection.
- Do not sand or grind old coatings without a respirator; old paint dust can
  contain lead and chromate, both toxic and carcinogenic.
- Lead-acid batteries and chromate-bearing primers are hazardous. Treat every
  coating as unknown until it has been tested.
- Zinc and magnesium anodes release hydrogen gas as they corrode. Never store
  them in an enclosed space.
- Grinding sparks and hot metal are a fire hazard near fuel or a battery.
  Clear the area first.
- Work on any structure under tension or pressure only with appropriate
  training, supervision, and equipment.
