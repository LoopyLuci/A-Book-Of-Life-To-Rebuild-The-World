# Combustion and Elemental Analysis

> **Domain:** chemistry | **Difficulty:** journeyman | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Combustion analysis burns a weighed sample in a stream of oxygen and weighs
the carbon dioxide and water produced, which gives the carbon and hydrogen
content by difference. It remains the reference method for checking a fuel's
heating value and the composition of an organic material. The key principle is
that every carbon atom leaves as CO2 and every hydrogen atom leaves as H2O, so
the mass of the products fixes the mass of the elements in the sample.

## Prerequisites

- Formal training in analytical chemistry
- Access to a tube furnace and a balance reading to 0.1 mg
- Prior work with high-purity oxygen and with absorption trains
- A fume hood and appropriate supervision for the work described here

## Materials & Tools

- Combustion tube of borosilicate glass or fused silica with ground joints
- Tube furnace holding 800 to 1000 °C with a temperature controller
- High-purity oxygen with a regulator and a flow meter
- Copper(II) oxide or platinum gauze as the oxidiser
- Absorption tube of anhydrous calcium chloride for the water
- Absorption train of soda lime or potassium hydroxide for the CO2
- Analytical balance reading to 0.1 mg, with certified weights
- Alumina or platinum sample boats
- Tygon or copper tubing, stopcocks, and a bubble flow meter

## Procedure

### Stage 1: Condition the apparatus

1. Heat the empty tube at 800 °C with oxygen flowing for 30 minutes to burn
   off contamination.
2. Fill the water absorption tube with fresh anhydrous calcium chloride and
   the CO2 tube with fresh soda lime, and weigh each to 0.1 mg.
3. Leak-check the train and set the oxygen flow, typically 200 to 400 mL per
   minute for a bench tube.

### Stage 2: Burn the sample

1. Weigh 20 to 50 mg of sample into a pre-weighed boat and push it into the
   hot zone.
2. Hold the furnace at 800 to 900 °C and keep the oxygen flow steady for the
   whole burn.
3. Keep the burn slow enough that the oxygen downstream of the tube never
   runs out; a violet flame at the outlet means the flow is too high.

### Stage 3: Weigh the products

1. Cool the absorption tubes to room temperature in a desiccator and reweigh
   them.
2. Run a blank burn with no sample and use it to correct the weights for
   water and carbon dioxide picked up from the air.

### Stage 4: Compute the composition

1. Calculate the mass of CO2 and of water from the corrected weights.
2. Derive the carbon mass as 12.011 divided by 44.009 of the CO2, and the
   hydrogen mass as 2.016 divided by 18.015 of the water.
3. Report the percentages and the residual, which is usually oxygen,
   nitrogen, or ash, and check that the total reaches 100 percent.
4. Run a duplicate sample and require agreement within about 0.3 percent
   absolute before trusting the result.

## Safety Notes

- Hot quartz tubes at 900 °C cause severe burns and shatter on thermal shock.
  Let the furnace cool before you open it.
- Oxygen supports vigorous combustion. Keep oil, grease, and solvents away
  from the train, and never use an open flame with oxygen flowing.
- An unknown sample can release toxic decomposition products. Carry out this
  work only with appropriate training, supervision, and equipment, in a fume
  hood.
- Absorption tubes are fragile and give false results if handled carelessly.
  Use gloves and never touch the absorbing surfaces.
- Soda lime and calcium chloride are irritating dusts. Wear a respirator when
  changing the absorbers.
- Oxygen cylinders are a serious hazard. Secure them upright with the cap in
  place and use a regulator, never an improvised fitting.
