# Ship Safety Standards and Manning

> **Domain:** ship | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

SOLAS and the flag state set the floor: what a vessel must have
aboard, what her crew must be able to do, and what must be proved by
survey and certificate. The key principle is that the certificate is
the evidence of readiness, not the readiness itself, so the drills, the
muster list, and the maintenance that keep the certificate valid
matter as much as the paperwork. A vessel that cannot muster, cannot
fight a fire, and cannot abandon ship safely is not seaworthy whatever
paper it carries.

## Prerequisites

- Familiarity with the layout and the systems of the vessel
- Basic seamanship: steering, anchoring, and the use of the radio
- Fire fighting and survival at sea, and first aid
- The Collision Regulations and the distress procedure
- Reading a muster list and a drill schedule, and keeping a drill log

## Materials & Tools

- The vessel's safety certificates and the safety management system
  documents
- Crew list, the safe manning document, and the watchkeeping records
- Lifejackets, life rafts, lifebuoys, and the hydrostatic release units
- EPIRB, SART, VHF with DSC, and the portable radios
- Fire extinguishers, fire hoses, smoke hoods, and the fixed extinguishing
  system
- Muster list, the emergency checklist, and the drill log
- Liferaft service certificates and the maintenance log for the
  launching gear

## Procedure

### Understand the requirements

1. Identify the flag state, the class society, and the category of the
   vessel, and confirm that the construction standard and the carriage
   requirements apply to that category.
2. List the certificates the vessel must hold, such as the safety
   construction, the safety equipment, the load line, and the radio
   licence, and check the expiry of each one.
3. Match the required carriage of life-saving appliances, fire
   appliances, and navigational equipment to what is actually aboard,
   and record any shortfall.

### Survey and certify

1. Schedule the annual survey and the dry-dock survey, and give the
   surveyor access to the hull, the machinery, the safety equipment, and
   the records.
2. Keep the defect list and the survey record, and close each defect
   with evidence rather than with a promise.
3. Maintain the safety management system with a document of compliance
   and a safety management certificate, and hold the internal audits
   and the management review on schedule.

### Man the vessel

1. Set the watch schedule so that a proper look-out is always on deck,
   and record the hours of rest: no more than 10 hours in any 24 hours
   and no more than 77 hours in any 7 days.
2. Assign the watchkeeping and the engineering duties to certificated
   officers who hold the required certificates of competency.
3. Rotate the duties so that no officer keeps a night watch alone in a
   dangerous trade, and brief every joining crew member on the
   emergency duties before they stand their first watch.

### Drill the emergencies

1. Run the fire, the abandon-ship, the man-overboard, and the damage
   control drills at the intervals set in the safety management system,
   and set the scenarios and the alarms in advance.
2. Muster the crew by the muster list, count the crew, and name the
   muster stations in advance so that the boat assignments are known.
3. Check that each person can reach their station and their equipment
   in the dark, and record the time taken and the defects found.

## Safety Notes

- A certificate never replaces a working drill; the certificate can be
  valid while the crew is unable to launch the raft or find the fire
  hose.
- Abandon-ship and fire drills are hazardous in themselves: the
  greatest injuries during a drill come from cold water, from falls on
  a wet deck, and from the boat swinging against the hull. Conduct
  them only with appropriate training, supervision, and equipment.
- Fire is the hazard that kills most quickly at sea. Keep the escape
  routes, the hatch closers, and the fire door seals intact, and never
  seal a compartment that is not in its designed condition.
- A fixed gas system in an engine room or a paint locker can kill in
  seconds without an atmosphere test; never enter a space that has
  been closed, and follow the shutdown and the lock-out procedure.
- Liferaft and lifejacket service dates and hydrostatic release
  replacements are the difference between a raft that inflates and one
  that does not; log every expiry date and check it against the
  calendar.
- Keep the emergency muster list posted and current, and make sure
  every crew member knows the boat they would board.
