# Cell Biology and Microscopy

> **Domain:** biology | **Difficulty:** novice | **Safety:** green

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Every living thing is built from cells, and a microscope turns an opaque smear
of tissue into visible structure. Knowing cell structure and preparing slides
well is the difference between seeing bacteria and seeing only stained debris.
The key principle is that most specimens are transparent, so contrast has to
come from stains, from phase contrast, or from the optics of the instrument.

## Prerequisites

- Safe use of a compound light microscope
- Making a simple wet mount
- Handling a specimen without contaminating it or yourself

## Materials & Tools

- Compound light microscope with 4x, 10x, 40x, and 100x objectives, a
  movable stage, and a lamp
- Slides, cover slips, a dropper, and a lens tissue
- Methylene blue or crystal violet stain, dilute iodine, and India ink
- Cotton swabs, toothpicks, and sterile sample swabs
- Covered containers for specimens, and gloves
- Immersion oil for the 100x objective
- Hand lens of 10x for a gross look before you mount anything
- Notebook for drawings and counts

## Procedure

### Stage 1: Set up and focus the microscope

1. Place the microscope on a stable bench with the lamp at the back and the
   objectives turned away from you.
2. Switch on the lamp, raise the stage, and bring the 4x objective into
   position over the specimen.
3. Move the slide until the specimen is in the lit area, focus with the coarse
   knob and then the fine knob, then centre the specimen and step up through
   10x to 40x, raising the lamp brightness as you go.

### Stage 2: Prepare a wet mount

1. Place a drop of water on a clean slide and use a swab or toothpick to take a
   very small amount of the sample.
2. Stir the sample gently in the drop to spread it, then lower a cover slip at
   an angle so the air is pushed out rather than trapped.
3. Add one drop of stain at the edge of the cover slip and draw it through
   with blotting paper on the opposite side.

### Stage 3: Show specific structures

1. To reveal nuclei and cell walls, use methylene blue at about 0.1 percent
   and stain for 60 seconds; for starch and other granules, use dilute
   iodine, which turns blue-black where starch is present.
2. For a negative stain, mix a dilute cell suspension with India ink and
   observe the unstained cells as bright shapes against a dark ground.

### Stage 4: Record what you see

1. Draw the field of view at 400x and mark the scale bar, or the measured
   field diameter for that objective.
2. Note the arrangement of the cells, whether a cell wall is present, and
   whether the cells are moving, then repeat on a fresh sample.

### Stage 5: Care for the instrument

1. Lower the stage fully, remove the slide, and wipe each objective with lens
   tissue after use.
2. Cover the microscope, switch off the lamp, and store it in a dry case.

## Safety Notes

- Never look down the eyepiece while raising the stage or swinging an
  objective in. It will drive the slide into your eye.
- The 100x objective needs immersion oil. Use it only with the correct
  cover slip, and wipe the oil off afterwards or it will gum up the optics.
- Microscope lamps get hot and can scorch paper and benches. Keep the
  instrument on a stable, clear surface.
- Use a fresh slide and a fresh swab for every specimen so you do not spread
  contamination between samples.
- Handle clinical or unknown biological specimens with gloves, and only with
  appropriate training, supervision, and equipment. Do not eat or drink at
  the bench.
- Do not incubate or grow unknown material in an open room. Work with a
  supervisor and follow the site's containment rules.
