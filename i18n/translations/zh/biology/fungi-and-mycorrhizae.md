# Fungi, Mushrooms and Mycorrhizae

> **Domain:** biology | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Fungi recycle dead matter into soil and trade minerals for plant sugars
through mycorrhizal partnerships at the root. The mushroom is only the
fruiting body; the organism is the mycelium spreading through the soil.
The key principle: learn the mycelium, not just the cap, because that is
the part doing the work.

## Prerequisites

- Soil Biology and Composting
- A local guide to the edible and poisonous fungi of the region
- Basic plant identification, to link fungi to their host trees

## Materials & Tools

- A hand lens of 10x and a printed field guide
- A camera, for photographs of the cap, stem, and gills in place
- A small knife and a clean board for cutting specimens
- White and black paper, a glass slide, a cup, and a bowl for spore prints
- A microscope at 400x for measuring spores
- Plastic bags and a marker for separating individual collections
- A hand trowel for soil sampling around the roots of a host tree
- Leaf mould and wood chips for mulching mycorrhizal plantings

## Procedure

### Identify a Specimen Safely

1. Photograph the specimen in place, including the cap, the stem base, and
   any ring or volva.
2. Record the substrate, the nearby trees, the date, and the gill
   attachment: free, attached, notched, or descending.
3. Compare every feature against the guide, including the spore print
   colour, before anything is eaten.

### Take a Spore Print

1. Remove the cap and lay it gills-down on white and black paper, or on a
   glass slide, then cover it with a bowl.
2. Leave it undisturbed for 6 to 12 hours in still, humid air.
3. Lift the cap and the paper together, read the print colour in daylight,
   and measure the spores under 400x if a microscope is available.

### Harvest for Food

1. Cut the specimen cleanly at the base, leave the mycelium in the ground,
   and take less than a third of what you can see.
2. Keep each collection in its own bag, because one poisonous cap spoils a
   basket.
3. Cook thoroughly, never eat a wild mushroom raw, and learn the two or
   three edible species that grow near you before widening the search.

### Maintain Mycorrhizal Associations

1. Plant host species that suit both the climate and the local fungal
   community, and keep the root zone planted and undisturbed.
2. Avoid deep tilling, which severs the hyphae that feed the roots, and
   skip high-phosphorus fertiliser, which suppresses the partnership.
3. Mulch with leaf litter or wood chips to feed the fungi, and spread
   spore-bearing mulch from a healthy stand onto bare ground.

## Safety Notes

- Mushroom poisoning is a medical emergency; keep the specimen so it can be
  identified, and seek medical help at once.
- Never taste a wild mushroom to test it; taste tests are how people die.
- Confusion between edible and deadly look-alikes is common, so treat any
  uncertain cap as poisonous.
- Spores from old, dried, or mouldy specimens can trigger respiratory
  allergy; do not inhale them.
- Some species irritate skin and gut; wash hands and do not handle spoiled
  or rotting material bare-handed.
- Do not collect where spray or herbicide has been used, or within the
  required buffer of roadsides and treated land.
