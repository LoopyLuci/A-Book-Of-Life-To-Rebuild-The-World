# Electroplating and Anodizing

> **Domain:** chemistry | **Difficulty:** journeyman | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Electroplating puts a controlled metal layer on a part, and anodizing grows
a hard oxide layer on aluminium. Both are electrochemical, so both are
governed by current, time, and surface preparation. The key principle is
Faraday's law: the mass deposited depends only on current, time, and the
number of electrons involved.

## Prerequisites

- Redox Chemistry and Electrochemical Cells
- Metal Smelting, in the materials domain

## Materials & Tools

- A bench DC power supply, 0 to 30 V with a current limit
- Plating bath, anode of the plating metal, and steel cathode racks
- Workpieces, fine abrasive, degreaser, and a dilute acid pickle
- Masking tape and stop-off lacquer for parts to be left bare
- Anhydrous sodium sulfate for drying organic-rinse films
- A thickness gauge or a simple weighing method for copper on steel

## Procedure

### Stage 1: Prepare the Surface

1. Degrease the part in warm alkaline cleaner and rinse in water.
2. Pickle in 10 percent hydrochloric acid for 5 to 30 seconds to strip
   oxide, then rinse in clean water.
3. Work within a few minutes of pickling, because the surface re-tarnishes.
4. For steel, dip in a zinc activation solution before plating.
5. Handle with clean gloves and avoid fingerprints on the face.

### Stage 2: Set Up the Cell

1. Make the workpiece the cathode and the plating metal the anode.
2. Space the anodes 10 to 15 cm from the workpiece for even coverage.
3. Fill the bath so the anode stays immersed and the workpiece is covered.
4. Lower the work into the bath, then tap the rack to shake off bubbles.
5. Set the voltage first, then close the current control.

### Stage 3: Plate

1. Start at a low current and raise it over one to two minutes.
2. Hold a current density of 5 to 20 mA per square centimetre.
3. Keep the bath between 20 and 30 °C and agitate it gently.
4. Plate 10 to 25 micrometres for wear resistance on steel parts.
5. Watch the work: a dark, burnt deposit means the current density is too
   high, and heavy bubbling on the part means too much current.

### Stage 4: Anodize

1. Degrease, then etch in 10 percent sodium hydroxide at 60 °C for 30 to
   60 minutes to remove the rolling skin.
2. Make the aluminium part the anode and a lead or graphite cathode.
3. Use 15 to 20 percent sulfuric acid at 20 °C in a cooled tank.
4. Hold 10 to 20 V for 20 to 60 minutes, watching for a colour shift.
5. Seal by immersing in boiling water for 20 minutes, then dry.

### Stage 5: Inspect and Troubleshoot

1. Measure coating thickness on a hidden area or by weighing.
2. Burnt, dark deposits mean the current density was too high.
3. Pits and poor coverage mean the part was not cleaned properly.
4. Peeling means the substrate was oxide-contaminated before plating.
5. Dull, patchy films mean the bath is contaminated or starved.

## Safety Notes

- Sulfuric acid and sodium hydroxide are corrosive; wear face shield,
  acid-resistant gloves, and an apron.
- Anodizing baths reach 60 °C and splash; keep the tank covered when idle.
- Hydrogen evolves at the cathode and can ignite; keep flames away and
  ventilate the area.
- Plating baths give off acid mist; work in a hood or a well-ventilated
  bench.
- Never use cyanide plating baths, and avoid hexavalent chromium. Use
  sulphate or trivalent chromium baths instead, with appropriate training,
  supervision, and equipment.
- Cyanide salts are acutely lethal, and hexavalent chromium is a known
  carcinogen; both require a dedicated facility and a trained operator.
