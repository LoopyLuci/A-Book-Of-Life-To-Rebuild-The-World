# Laboratory Safety and Small-Scale Synthesis

> **Domain:** chemistry | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Most laboratory accidents come from a short list of causes: the wrong scale,
the wrong container, the wrong order of addition, and no plan for the waste.
Small-scale synthesis done under control turns a bench and a few pieces of
glassware into kilograms of useful material. The key principle is to scale
down until the hazard is smaller than your ability to control it, and never
mix two protocols that were not designed to work together.

## Prerequisites

- Prior laboratory induction and a written procedure for the reaction
- The safety data sheet for every reagent in the procedure
- A hazard assessment that names the worst credible outcome of each step
- A labelled waste plan for every stream you will generate

## Materials & Tools

- Written procedure with reagent quantities and a hazard assessment
- Splash goggles, lab coat or apron, closed shoes, and gloves matched to the
  reagent
- Fume hood or a well-ventilated bench, with the sash kept low
- Beakers, conical flasks, and round-bottom flasks rated for the heat and
  pressure involved
- Balance, graduated cylinders, and volumetric pipettes
- Stirrer, hot plate, ice bath, and thermometer
- Spill kit, fire blanket, suitable extinguisher, and a working eyewash
- Labelled waste containers for acid, base, solvent, and heavy metal streams
- Logbook for lot numbers, additions, temperatures, and yields

## Procedure

### Stage 1: Plan before you start

1. Read the whole procedure and the safety data sheet for every reagent
   before you touch anything.
2. Scale the reaction down until the smallest useful quantity still leaves
   the largest single hazard at least ten times smaller than the vessel
   limits, and scale down again if you are unsure.
3. Write down what happens at each step if it goes wrong, and what you will do
   about it.

### Stage 2: Set up the workspace

1. Gather every reagent and every piece of equipment before starting, and
   check each label against the procedure one by one.
2. Work in a fume hood for anything volatile, toxic, or dusty, and clear the
   bench of clutter so the route to the exit, the eyewash, and the
   extinguisher stays clear.

### Stage 3: Work the reaction

1. Add reagents in the order the procedure states. When adding acid to water,
   always add the acid to the water slowly while stirring, never the other
   way round.
2. Use the lowest stirrer speed that mixes well, and add any gas-evolving
   reagent last and in small portions.
3. Hold the vessel at the scale and rate the procedure specifies, and never
   heat a closed container.
4. Record every addition with its time and the temperature. A timestamped
   log is what makes an accident reconstructable afterwards.

### Stage 4: Work up and dispose

1. Cool the mixture, then quench it gradually with stirring, following the
   quench stated in the procedure.
2. Separate and wash the product, transfer it to a labelled container, and
   weigh it to get the actual yield.
3. Segregate the waste into the correct labelled streams, and never pour
   anything down a drain unless the local rules and the procedure allow it.

## Safety Notes

- Add acid to water, never water to acid. Adding water to concentrated acid
  can boil the mixture and throw acid across the bench.
- Concentrated acids and bases are severely corrosive. Goggles and gloves are
  the minimum, and add a face shield for decanting.
- Flammable solvents must be kept away from ignition sources. Use an
  electric hot plate, never a flame.
- Never heat a sealed system or a filled vessel. A blocked condenser or a
  runaway exotherm will pressurise and burst the glass.
- Many organic reagents are carcinogens, sensitisers, or neurotoxins. Work
  only with appropriate training, supervision, and equipment, and read the
  safety data sheet rather than relying on memory.
- Unknown or untested mixtures can ignite, decompose, or explode. If you
  cannot identify the hazards, do not proceed.
- Dispose of waste properly. Reaction leftovers in a drain can release
  hydrogen sulfide, contaminate with heavy metals, or start a fire in the
  sewer.
- Know your emergency actions before you start: where the eyewash, the spill
  kit, and the extinguisher are, how to raise the alarm, and where the
  assembly point is.
