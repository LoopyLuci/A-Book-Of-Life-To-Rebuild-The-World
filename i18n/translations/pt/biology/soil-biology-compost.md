# Soil Biology and Composting

> **Domain:** biology | **Difficulty:** novice | **Safety:** green

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Soil is a living system in which bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and worms turn
dead matter into plant nutrients. Composting is that process under control,
and getting the carbon to nitrogen ratio, the moisture, and the temperature
right turns waste into a soil amendment. The key principle: compost is
finished when the pile stops heating and no longer smells of ammonia.

## Prerequisites

- A basic understanding of plant nutrients and soil texture
- Space for a pile, with water and a fork or turner to hand
- A thermometer long enough to reach the centre of the pile

## Materials & Tools

- Dry brown material: straw, shredded cardboard, dry leaves, small twigs
- Green material: grass clippings, vegetable scraps without meat, manure
- Water and a hose or watering can
- A pile thermometer, a pitchfork, and a tarp
- Screening wire and a bucket for the finished compost

## Procedure

### Building the Pile

1. Choose a shaded, well-drained site and build a heap roughly 1 m by 1 m
   by 1.5 m for a small batch.
2. Lay a base of coarse sticks 10 cm deep so that air can move beneath the
   pile.
3. Alternate 15 cm layers of brown and green material, and mix in a
   handful of finished compost as the starter.
4. Aim for a carbon to nitrogen ratio of about 25 to 30 parts carbon to one
   part nitrogen; greens alone run near 10 to 15 and browns near 50 to 100.
5. Water each layer until the material feels like a wrung-out sponge, at
   roughly 40 to 60 percent moisture.

### Managing the Pile

1. Check the centre temperature daily; a healthy pile reaches 55 to 65
   degrees C within a week.
2. Hold the pile in that range for at least 3 days, or turn it 5 times and
   hold it for 15 days, so the heat kills most weed seeds and pathogens.
3. Turn the pile when it cools, or when the centre stays more than 10
   degrees C above the edges, which shows the edges are too dry.
4. Re-wet dry patches, and add more brown material if the pile smells of
   ammonia or turns sour.

### Testing and Harvesting

1. Expect a fast pile to finish in 6 to 8 weeks and a slow one in 3 to 6
    months; check that the pile no longer heats after turning and smells of
    earth rather than ammonia.
2. Squeeze a handful: it should hold together and then break apart when
    pressed, not form a dense ball.
3. Sieve the compost to 10 to 15 mm, test it in a seed tray with radish or
    lettuce for normal germination in 4 to 7 days, and store the finished
    compost under cover for up to a year.

## Safety Notes

- A working pile reaches 65 degrees C and will burn skin; probe it with a
  long thermometer and never put your hand into the centre.
- Decomposing material can produce carbon monoxide in a sealed space, so
  keep the pile outdoors or in a well-ventilated shed.
- Animal manure and kitchen scraps can carry pathogens such as E. coli and
  Salmonella; do not use this compost on edible crops close to harvest.
- Dry compost dust irritates the lungs; wear a mask when sieving or
  handling a dusty pile.
- Honey bees and other insects swarm warm compost; do not site the pile near
  an apiary.
- Keep the pile away from wells and watercourses so that nutrient-rich
  runoff cannot reach streams.
