# Insects and Pollinators

> **Domain:** biology | **Difficulty:** novice | **Safety:** green

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Insects are the pollinators that move pollen between flowers and the
decomposers that move nutrients through soil. Building habitat for them
and protecting colonies from pesticides is one of the cheapest ways to raise
crop yield. The key principle: a farm needs forage across the whole season,
so something must be blooming in every month of it.

## Prerequisites

- A basic knowledge of insect life cycles
- A survey of the land and the vegetation already on it
- Materials for nest boxes and for a planted border

## Materials & Tools

- Bee boxes or a top-bar hive with frames
- Nesting bundles of hollow bamboo, reed stems, or drilled wood blocks
- Bare ground and mud for solitary ground-nesting bees
- Flower seed for a mixed border with early and late bloomers
- A water source with shallow landing stones
- Insect hotels of stacked wood blocks, pipe, and cardboard tubes
- Protective gear: veil, gloves, smoker, and a hive tool
- Record sheets for colony counts and garden pollinator counts

## Procedure

### Understanding the Life Cycle

1. Map the stages: egg, larva, pupa, and adult for complete metamorphosis,
   or egg and nymph for incomplete metamorphosis.
2. Note the scale of a colony: 60 000 honey bee workers make about 2
   million foraging trips a day, each visit to a single flower.
3. Watch the brood cycle of about 21 days from egg to emerging adult in the
   honey bee, and time the work to the local foraging calendar rather than
   to the calendar month.

### Providing Habitat

1. Provide the three resources in balance: about 40 percent bare ground
   for ground-nesting bees, 30 percent hollow cavities, and 30 percent
   hollow stems.
2. Site hives in a sheltered spot with morning sun, out of the prevailing
   wind, with water within a few hundred metres.
3. Add brood boxes in steps; a colony needs roughly 0.5 to 1 cubic metre
   of space as it grows through the season.
4. Fill nesting bundles with hollow stems of 5 to 10 mm diameter and clear
   them each spring, and drill holes 5 to 8 mm across and 10 cm deep into
   a block of untreated wood for solitary bees.
5. Plant a mixed border that gives at least three species in bloom in
   every month, with both a nectar source and a pollen source.

### Protecting the Colony

1. Inspect for varroa mites, and requeen a colony that shows a spotty brood
   pattern.
2. Plan for the swarm season: a colony may leave with 5 to 10 kg of bees
    and half its strength.
3. Do not spray insecticides while crops are blooming, and if spraying is
    unavoidable, treat at night and leave an unsprayed refuge of at least a
    third of the land.
4. Prefer insecticidal soaps and oils over broad-spectrum insecticides,
    keep them out of the bloom, reduce mowing and tillage, leave
    uncultivated strips through the winter, and keep a year-on-year count
    of colonies and pollinator visits so the trend stays visible.

## Safety Notes

- Stings cause allergic reactions and anaphylaxis in a small number of
  people; keep hives at least 25 metres from public paths and wear a veil
  and gloves.
- Never open a honey bee colony without protective clothing, a clear exit
  to the rear, and a smoker, since stings become more likely in bad
  weather.
- Many insects carry disease; never handle stinging insects bare-handed,
  and never apply an insecticide without reading the label and wearing the
  protection it names.
- Insecticides are toxic to people, pets, and aquatic life; lock them
  away, never decant them into drink bottles, and respect the harvest
  interval on the label.
- Old nests, bee debris, and rotten wood carry fungal spores and mites;
  wear a mask when disturbing old insect habitat.
- Check local rules and the landowner's permission before moving hives or
  collecting wild nests.
