# Thermochemistry and Calorimetry

> **Domain:** chemistry | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Calorimetry measures heat by watching a temperature change, and thermochemistry
is the bookkeeping that turns those readings into enthalpies. It lets you size
a fire, judge the fuel value of a wood lot, and rate the output of a stove or
a chemical reaction. The key principle is q = m · c · ΔT, so the heat held by
a body is its mass times its specific heat capacity times its temperature
change.

## Prerequisites

- Basic algebra and careful unit handling
- A balance and a thermometer that you know how to check
- A clear heat source and a fire extinguisher within reach

## Materials & Tools

- Calorimeter: nested polystyrene cups, or a cup in a wooden cradle, or a
  Dewar flask
- Lid with a hole for the thermometer and a stir rod
- Thermometer reading to 0.1 °C, covering -10 to 110 °C
- Balance reading to 0.01 g, or a kitchen scale to 1 g
- Heat source: spirit burner, hot plate, or immersion heater
- Sample and cup masses recorded before the run
- Tongs, a heatproof mat, and a fire extinguisher

## Procedure

### Stage 1: Calibrate the instruments

1. Check the thermometer in ice water at 0 °C and in boiling water at about
   100 °C at sea level, less at altitude.
2. Weigh the empty dry calorimeter and record the mass to the resolution of
   the balance.
3. Weigh the sample and the water you will add, and record both.

### Stage 2: Run the measurement

1. Fit the lid, thermometer, and stir rod so the bulb sits in the liquid
   without touching the wall.
2. Start with the sample and the water at the same measured temperature, or
   use the cold-cup method for an exothermic reaction.
3. Stir gently and record the temperature every 30 seconds until it stops
   changing, keeping the lid closed throughout.
4. Repeat with a second, larger sample and confirm the energy per gram comes
   out consistent.

### Stage 3: Compute the heat

1. Take ΔT as the measured start temperature minus the peak temperature.
2. Compute q = m · c · ΔT with c = 4.18 J/(g·°C) for water, and add the
   calorimeter's own heat capacity if it is known, about 1 to 2 J/(g·°C) for a
   polystyrene cup.
3. Divide by the sample mass for specific energy in J/g, then divide by 4184
   to convert to kJ/g.

### Stage 4: Read a heating curve

1. Plot temperature against time and mark the plateau where the phase change
   absorbs heat without a rise in temperature.
2. Use the length of the plateau and the known heater power to estimate the
   latent heat of the change.

## Safety Notes

- A flame is a real fire risk. Keep an extinguisher close, keep the lid and
  the mat within reach, and never leave the burner unattended.
- Hot liquid and hot glassware cause burns. Use tongs and a heatproof mat
  for every transfer.
- Never stir by lifting the stir rod clear of the liquid while it is spinning;
  it splashes hot solution.
- Never heat a sealed container, and never heat an empty vessel.
- Do not eat or drink anything that has been in the calorimeter. Label it and
  keep food out of the area.
- Allow the thermometer and the balance to return to room temperature before
  reading them, or the numbers will drift.
