# Shipyard Layout and Material Flow

> **Domain:** ship | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** green

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

A shipyard is an arrangement of sheds, yards, and cranes in which steel
travels in one direction and a hull grows from a line on the slipway to a
finished vessel at the quay. The key principle is flow: material should
move the shortest practical distance and always in the same direction, and
no operation should require a plate to cross a road or a crane path twice.
A yard that mixes its steps is slow and dangerous, and the layout is what
decides both.

## Prerequisites

- The build sequence for the class of vessel to be built
- Basic industrial layout: zones, flows, and handling distances
- Reading a hull lines plan and an arrangement drawing
- Rough estimating of area, crane demand, and labour
- Fire separation and traffic planning for an industrial site

## Materials & Tools

- A scaled site plan of the yard and the berths
- The build sequence, the berth schedule, and the delivery schedule
- Crane list with reach, capacity, and hook height
- Plate and profile stock records and a stocktaking sheet
- A spreadsheet for areas, distances, and handling volumes
- Drawing board, tape, and graph paper for the layout study
- A fire plan with the hydrants, extinguishers, and muster points
- Marking paint, barriers, and signage for the traffic routes

## Procedure

### Lay out the flow

1. Write the build sequence down: stock, cutting, forming, assembly,
   welding, blasting and painting, outfitting, launch, fitting out, and
   trials.
2. Place the zones in that order, with the stockyard at one end and the
   outfitting berth at the other, so that no material travels backwards.
3. Size each shed for the work in it; a welding shop needs floor area for
   the largest block, with about 1.5 to 2 times the block footprint for
   handling and for the jigs.
4. Provide a straight route from the stockyard to the slip, wide enough
   for two loaded trucks to pass, about 6 m for a one-way flow.

### Fit the cranes and the berth

1. Match the crane reach to the hull: every point of the largest hull
   must lie inside the working radius of some crane, with the reach at
   least the breadth of the hull plus 5 m of working space.
2. Check the capacity against the worst case: the heaviest single lift,
   the block or the mast unit, must be inside the capacity at the radius
   where it is picked, not at the crane's best reach.
3. Size the slip and the berth: the slip must be longer than the length
   overall plus its ramp, and the lay-up berth about 1.2 to 1.5 times the
   length overall for a single vessel.
4. Put the outfitting shops along the berth with short access, since
   outfitting moves a very large number of small items over a long
   distance.

### Control the stock

1. Lay the plate stockyard flat on dunnage with the grades separated and
   the heat numbers facing the aisle.
2. Issue material only against a cutting list, so that the offcuts are
   known and can be reused, and carry the heat number onto every part cut
   from the plate.
3. Keep 3 to 5 days of steel consumption in stock to cover a late
   delivery, and no more, because a full yard is a stale yard.
4. Sort the profile stock by length and cut the longest orders first.

### Manage the site

1. Mark the walkways and the vehicle routes and keep them apart, so that
   the cranes, the trucks, and the welders never have to share a path.
2. Separate the paint shop from the hot shop by fire separation, and put
   the paint store outside the building and downwind of the blasting area.
3. Enclose the blasting area with dust extraction, since the dust from a
   coated surface is a respirable hazard.
4. Publish the fire plan, the hydrant and extinguisher points, the
   muster point, and the emergency shut-offs for the gas and the power.

## Safety Notes

- Traffic is the everyday risk: separate the pedestrians, the forklifts,
  and the overhead crane loads, and mark the routes on the ground.
- Never work under a suspended load and never travel a crane over people.
- Keep the plate stacks stable with dunnage and chocks, and set a stack
  height limit that the yard enforces.
- The paint shop is the most likely place for a fire to start: keep it
  separated, keep the flammables outside, and keep the extinguishers in
  the aisle.
- Blasting and painting produce dust and vapour; ventilate, and use the
  right respiratory protection.
- Keep the slip and the ways clear, and never leave a vessel unattended
  on the slip in a way that lets it move.
- Provide a route out of every confined space and keep it clear.
