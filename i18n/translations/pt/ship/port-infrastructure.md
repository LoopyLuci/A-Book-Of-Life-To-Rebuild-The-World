# Ports, Harbours and Dredging

> **Domain:** ship | **Difficulty:** master | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

A harbour is a system of moving water made still enough to work in, and
every part of it is sized against the waves and the tide it has to
survive. The key principle is that the breakwater sets the design
wave, the wave sets the armour size, the depth sets the dredging, and
the silt sets the maintenance cycle; get one of them wrong and the
cost moves to the next one. A port that is not planned for the
sediment it creates will silt up and be dredged forever.

## Prerequisites

- Coastal engineering: waves, tides, sediment transport, and refraction
  and diffraction
- Geotechnical site investigation and the soil parameters from it
- Hydrographic survey capability and the ability to run a dredging
  calculation
- The vessel dimensions and the manoeuvring requirements of the traffic
- Statutory control of the works and the local permitting regime
- Cost estimation for marine works, and a bill of quantities

## Materials & Tools

- Wave climate data and a design significant wave height from a
  1-in-100-year return period analysis
- Rubble armour graded by weight, and a quarry source with a rock
  quality test
- Core material of graded quarry run, and geotextile filter fabric
- Dredging plant: a cutter suction, a backhoe, a bucket ladder, or a
  trailing suction hopper dredger
- Hydrographic survey: a multibeam echosounder, a tide gauge, and a
  positioning system
- Settling test equipment for the dredged material, and a sampling
  plan for contaminated sediment
- Concrete or steel for the quay, and a mooring analysis

## Procedure

### Fix the design environment

1. Establish the design wave height and period at the site, the
   astronomical and design tidal levels, and the currents from the
   offshore and the estuary data.
2. Run the refraction and diffraction studies for the harbour
   entrance, and identify where the wave height is highest so the
   armour can be sized there rather than uniformly.
3. Set the design levels: the chart datum, the dredged depth, and the
   crest level of the breakwater, each with its own freeboard and
   tolerance.

### Design the breakwater and the berths

1. Size the armour units with the Hudson relation W = gamma x D^3 /
   (S x L)^2, where W is the unit mass in tonnes, D the nominal
   diameter in metres, S the wave height and L the wave length in
   metres, and gamma about 0.02 to 0.03 for rock.
2. Lay the breakwater with a core of graded material under a graded
   filter layer, with armour slopes of about 1:1.5 on the seaward face
   and a crest above the still water level.
3. Set the berth depth as the design draft plus the required clearance
   plus an allowance for squat and trim, and check the berth length
   and the bollard spacing against the mooring analysis.

### Dredge and dispose

1. Survey the existing bathymetry and compute the volume to be removed
   to the design depth, with a dredging tolerance of about 0.15 m and
   an over-dredge of a further 0.3 m.
2. Dredge in panels, survey each panel as it is finished, and record
   the surface against the design depth before moving on.
3. Test the material for contamination before it is moved, and dispose
   of it in a permitted placement site or treat and cap it in place.
4. Monitor the silt returning to the basin during the works, and
   adjust the silt curtain or the trap so that it holds the material
   out of the channel.

### Manage the silt

1. Measure the bed level in the basin and in the approach at a fixed
   interval, usually annually, plot the rate of accretion against the
   design depth, and compute the volume of the maintenance dredging
   from that rate and the interval.
2. Budget the plant, the disposal, and the closure days against that
   volume, and use the monitoring data to choose between a cycle of
   dredging and a structural fix such as a silt trap, a longer
   approach, or a different basin layout.

## Safety Notes

- Dredging is among the most dangerous marine work: the cutter, the
  ladder, the suction pipe, and the propeller of a dredger can kill
  without warning, and barges capsize if they are loaded unevenly.
  Only with appropriate training, supervision, and equipment.
- Confined space and engulfment are the hidden hazards: a moored
  barge or a dredge hopper can fill with water and silt without any
  visible change at the deck, so test the atmosphere and the water
  level before anyone enters.
- The chemicals used in dredging, from the polymer that conditions the
  spoil to the wash used on a contaminated hopper, burn skin and eyes
  and must be handled with eye protection, gloves, and a chemical
  assessment.
- The hazard to the public is a vessel striking the works: mark the
  works with lights, floats, and a radar reflector, keep the channel
  clear, and keep the aids to navigation working.
- The dredged material may carry heavy metals or hydrocarbons, so
  protect the crew from the dust and the splash, and document the
  disposal.
- Silt and water are unstable; survey the wall stability and the
  bearing capacity of the quay before putting a crane or a hopper
  against it.
