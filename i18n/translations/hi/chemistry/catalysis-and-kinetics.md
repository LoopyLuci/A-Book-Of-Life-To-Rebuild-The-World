# Catalysis and Reaction Kinetics

> **Domain:** chemistry | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** green

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources

## Overview

A catalyst changes how fast a reaction runs without being consumed. Knowing
the rate law and the activation energy tells you how to make a reaction
faster, slower, or safer, and how to shift an equilibrium in the direction
you want. The key principle is that every reaction needs to climb an energy
barrier, and the catalyst builds a lower path over it.

## Prerequisites

- Stoichiometry and Limiting Reagents
- Acid-Base Equilibria and Buffering

## Materials & Tools

- Test tubes, a test tube rack, stopwatch, and a thermometer
- Water baths at several set temperatures
- A gas syringe or a gas collection bottle for volume measurements
- Manganese dioxide powder, iron filings, and copper wire as catalysts
- 3 percent hydrogen peroxide, and a 1 M sodium chloride solution
- Yeast and sugar solution for a fermentation rate study

## Procedure

### Stage 1: Measure a Rate

1. Fix the temperature and let the reagents reach it.
2. Mix the reactants so the clock starts at the moment of contact.
3. Time a measurable change such as gas volume, colour, or mass loss.
4. Repeat three times and average the times.
5. Record the temperature with every measurement.

### Stage 2: Determine the Rate Law

1. Start from a trial rate, rate = k[A]^m[B]^n.
2. Change one reactant concentration and keep everything else fixed.
3. Compare the initial rates; the ratio of rates gives the order.
4. Doubling a concentration and doubling the rate means first order.
5. Solve for the constant k with one trial rate and the fitted orders.

### Stage 3: Test the Activation Energy

1. Run the same reaction at 20, 30, 40, and 50 °C.
2. Plot the natural log of k against one over T in kelvin.
3. Read the slope, which equals -Ea divided by R.
4. Multiply the slope by -8.314 joules per mole per kelvin for Ea.
5. Expect a 10 K rise near 298 K to raise the rate by roughly a factor of
   two when the activation energy is near 50 kJ per mole.

### Stage 4: Shift the Equilibrium

1. Apply Le Chatelier's principle to each change you consider.
2. Raise the temperature to favour the endothermic direction.
3. Remove a product or add a reactant to drive the reaction forward.
4. Raise the pressure only when gas moles differ between the sides.
5. Confirm the shift by measuring, not by assuming, that the rate law holds.

## Safety Notes

- Hydrogen and oxygen mixtures are explosive; keep every flame at least
  30 cm away and use the smallest quantities that will work.
- Hydrogen peroxide is an oxidiser; keep it off skin, wood, and cloth.
- Do not seal a tube that is producing gas; pressure will build up.
- Warm glassware can shatter on a cold bench; warm it slowly and use a
  water bath rather than a flame for anything flammable.
- Catalysts such as manganese dioxide dust are a breathing hazard; handle
  them in a draught and wash your hands afterwards.
