# Acid-Base Equilibria and Buffering

> **Domain:** chemistry | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Almost every process that has to be controlled, from soil pH to battery
electrolyte, is an acid-base problem. Buffering is what keeps a solution
steady when acid or base is added. The key principle is that a weak acid and
its conjugate base hold each other in balance, so the pH resists change
until one of the pair runs out.

## Prerequisites

- Stoichiometry and Limiting Reagents

## Materials & Tools

- pH paper or a natural indicator such as red cabbage juice
- A pH meter with a glass electrode, if available
- 100 mL and 250 mL beakers, a graduated cylinder, and a stir rod
- Dilute hydrochloric acid, dilute sodium hydroxide, and distilled water
- Weak acids and their salts, such as acetic acid and sodium acetate

## Procedure

### Stage 1: Measure the pH

1. Stir the sample and rinse the electrode or paper with distilled water.
2. Read the pH; for paper, compare the colour against the chart.
3. Take two readings and average them if they differ by more than 0.2.
4. Record the temperature, because pH 7 is neutral only at 25 °C.

### Stage 2: Run a Titration

1. Pipette 25.0 mL of the analyte into a beaker and add 25 mL of water.
2. Add a few drops of indicator and stir continuously.
3. Add titrant from a burette in 1 mL steps until the end point.
4. Refine to 0.05 mL near the end point.
5. Calculate the concentration from the moles delivered.

### Stage 3: Build a Buffer

1. Choose a weak acid with a pKa near the pH you need.
2. Dissolve equal molar amounts of the acid and its sodium salt.
3. For acetic acid, pKa is 4.76; for the ammonium ion, pKa is 9.25.
4. Adjust the ratio to shift the pH up or down by the amount required.
5. Use the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation to set the ratio.

### Stage 4: Verify Buffer Capacity

1. Add a fixed small dose of strong acid to the buffer and record the pH.
2. Repeat with the same dose of strong base.
3. Compare the pH changes with an unbuffered sample of the same volume.
4. A good buffer changes by less than 0.2 pH units for that dose.
5. Work only within about one pH unit of the pKa.

## Safety Notes

- Acids and bases burn skin and eyes; wear goggles and chemical gloves.
- Always add acid to water, never water to acid.
- Work in a ventilated area and keep a bucket of water within reach.
- Never pipette by mouth and never inhale a vapour directly.
- Dispose of acid and base waste as separate streams, never mixed.
