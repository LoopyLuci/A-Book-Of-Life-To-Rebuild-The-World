# Marine Steam Engines and Boilers

> **Domain:** ship | **Difficulty:** master | **Safety:** red

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

The reciprocating steam engine and the boiler that feeds it are the classic
marine power plant, and they remain the right answer where the fuel is
dirty, where fuel is cheap, or where the plant has to be repaired in the
field. The key principle is that the two halves are one system: the boiler
makes the steam, the engine's valves admit it, and the level and the
chemistry of the boiler water decide whether the boiler survives the week.
A steam plant is not judged by its indicator diagram but by its water
level and its water chemistry.

## Prerequisites

- Thermodynamics to the level of the saturated steam tables
- Boiler engineering: firing, heat transfer, and feedwater treatment
- The mechanics of a reciprocating engine and of the valve gear
- The safety regulations for boilers and pressure vessels
- Confined space entry and the permit system

## Materials & Tools

- The boiler: the steam drum, the furnace, the tubes, the safety valves,
  the water level gauge, the blowdown valves, and the feed pumps
- The engine: the cylinder, the piston, the piston rod, the crosshead, the
  connecting rod, the crankshaft, the bearings, and the slide or piston
  valves with the eccentric gear
- Feedwater treatment: the dosing tank, the deaerator, and the test kits
- Boiler tools: a tube expander, a tube cutter, a drift, and a rivet snap
- A straightedge, a feeler gauge, and blue for checking the centre line
- The spares: two sets of the valves and the rings, and the injector tips

## Procedure

### Set the plant up

1. Set the working pressure and the machinery against the design: a
   marine auxiliary boiler usually runs at 6 to 8 bar and a main boiler
   for a reciprocating engine at 12 to 20 bar, where steam at 15 bar is
   about 198 degrees Celsius.
2. Set the safety valves: at least two on every boiler, each set to the
   permitted pressure and each able to lift the steam the boiler can make;
   the setting is sealed and locked, and only a competent person changes
   it.
3. Fit the water level gauge with a column at least three times the
   diameter of the gauge, with a drain at the bottom, and test the gauge
   and the far-side valve every watch.
4. Fit the bottom blowdown and the surface blowdown, and check that both
   operate.

### Fire and make steam

1. Light the fires with the dampers at the correct opening and raise the
   steam slowly, so that the shell and the tubes are not shocked.
2. Watch the combustion: the flame must be inside the furnace, and a
   smoky fire means too little air and it will soot the tubes.
3. Watch the water level and the steam space, and remember that a boiler
   fed too fast carries water into the engine and bends the piston rods.
4. Keep the firing rate even and the pressure within 5 percent of the
   working pressure; a falling pressure shows a fouled or a leaking
   surface.

### Work the engine

1. Start with the engine warm and the cylinders drained, and admit the
   starting steam gradually to avoid a hydraulic shock.
2. Watch the condenser's vacuum; about 0.9 bar below atmospheric at the
   design speed is normal, and a poor vacuum costs more fuel than a poor
   boiler.
3. Keep the cylinders and the lubricators clean, and treat a hot
   cylinder or a false ring as a fault to be found, not as a condition to
   be worked around.
4. Run between 75 and 90 percent of the rated power, and avoid long
   periods at a very low speed, which is bad for the boiler and for the
   condenser.

### Protect the boiler

1. Test the feedwater and the boiler water every watch: the total
   dissolved solids, the pH, the alkalinity, and the dissolved oxygen.
2. Keep the dissolved solids below about 3500 ppm for a low-pressure
   boiler, blow down to hold that value, and use a continuous surface
   blowdown where the load is steady.
3. Dose the chemical scavenger and the phosphate treatment by the
   feedwater rate, not by eye, and check the feedwater heater and the
   deaerator in the same watch.
4. Inspect the tube ends, the stays, the handholes, and the fireboxes, and
   remember that a tube leak from caustic or chlorinated feedwater can
   put a hole in the boiler.
5. Carry out the annual survey: the safety valves, the water gauges, the
   uptakes, the fireboxes, the internal inspection, and the hydrostatic
   test where the rules require it.

## Safety Notes

- A boiler is a pressure vessel that can kill: the stored energy in a
  15 bar steam space is enough to throw a boiler across a room. Work on
  it only with appropriate training, supervision, and equipment.
- Steam burns: at 15 bar the steam is 198 degrees Celsius and, when dry,
  it is invisible, so a leak looks like nothing until it hits you.
- Never let the water level fall to the top of the fire tubes, and never
  raise it above them; the plate will fail in minutes.
- Only two people may open a valve on a boiler that has been shut down:
  the one in charge and the one he sends.
- Test the safety valve regularly and never tamper with the setting.
- Keep the coal and the oil stores away from the boiler room, and keep the
  firing doors under control while the boiler is under pressure.
- An incomplete fire in a closed boiler room makes the room lethal with
  carbon monoxide; a portable detector is cheap insurance.
- The lagging on older boilers may contain asbestos, so do not disturb it
  without a survey.
- The boiler drum, the firebox, and the double bottom are all confined
  spaces: test the atmosphere and work to a permit.
