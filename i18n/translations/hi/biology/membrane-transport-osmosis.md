# Cell Membrane Transport and Osmosis

> **Domain:** biology | **Difficulty:** novice | **Safety:** green

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Membranes decide what enters and leaves a cell, and the same lipid film that
keeps a cell intact drives the diffusion experiments used in classrooms and
clinics. Osmosis explains why a wilted vegetable firms up in salt water and why
infusion fluids must match the patient's blood. The key principle is that
solutes and water move down their own concentration gradients across a
selectively permeable membrane, and only an energy-driven pump can move a
solute uphill.

## Prerequisites

- Basic chemistry and the ability to make solutions by mass
- A knife and a cutting board handled safely
- A place to store samples for an hour without disturbance

## Materials & Tools

- Potatoes, carrots, or a live aquatic plant
- Table salt and sugar
- Distilled water and tap water
- Balance accurate to 0.1 g, plus a kitchen scale or graduated cylinders
- Sharp knife, cork borer, and cutting board
- Cups or small beakers with lids, and labels
- Paper towels and a ruler
- Clock or watch
- Dialysis tubing and rubber bands, if available
- Neutral red indicator, if available, for the membrane test

## Procedure

### Stage 1: Prepare the solutions

1. Make at least four concentrations of a single solute, for example 0, 0.5,
   1.0, and 2.0 molar sodium chloride, recording each as a mass of solute per
   litre, and label every cup with its concentration and the date.
2. Use distilled water for one series and tap water for another to show the
   effect of solutes already dissolved.

### Stage 2: Prepare the tissue

1. Wash the potato or carrot, then cut equal cylinders of 25 mm diameter and
   5 mm thickness with the cork borer or the knife.
2. Weigh each cylinder, rinse the starch off the cut faces, then blot it dry
   and weigh it again so that any later change is unambiguous.

### Stage 3: Run the osmosis test

1. Place the cylinders in the solutions so that each is fully submerged, and
   label the cups to match.
2. Leave them for 30 to 60 minutes, then weigh each cylinder again and record
   the change.
3. Plot the percentage mass change against the solution concentration. The
   line crosses zero at the isotonic point.
4. Keep one cylinder in distilled water as a control.

### Stage 4: Investigate the membrane itself

1. Fill a dialysis tubing sachet with a concentrated sugar solution, close
   it, and suspend it in distilled water.
2. Compare the final volumes inside the tubing and in the surrounding liquid
   to show water moving while the solute stays behind, and add a drop of
   neutral red to see whether the indicator stays inside the membrane or
   diffuses through it.

### Stage 5: Watch a living cell membrane

1. Place a piece of red onion epidermis in 5 percent salt solution and
   observe plasmolysis under the microscope at 400x.
2. Replace the salt solution with distilled water and watch the cells become
   turgid again within a few minutes.

## Safety Notes

- The main hazards at the microscope bench are the 100x objective and the heat
  of the lamp. Follow the cell biology and microscopy entry for the detailed
  rules.
- Salt solutions at 2 molar and above irritate skin and eyes. Rinse any
  splash with plenty of water.
- Do not eat any of the plant material used in the experiment, and keep the
  knives, boards, and containers away from food preparation.
- The cork borer and the knife cause cuts. Use a cutting board, cut away from
  yourself, and carry the blades pointed down.
- Wash your hands after handling soil, plant surfaces, or pond water, which
  can carry soil-transmitted pathogens.
- Use clean water for the experiment. Contaminated water gives a wrong result
  and grows organisms during the wait.
- Keep the samples labelled and out of reach of children and animals, since
  concentrated salt solution is harmful if drunk.
