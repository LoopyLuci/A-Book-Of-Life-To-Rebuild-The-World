# Ecosystem Monitoring and Biodiversity

> **Domain:** biology | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** green

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Biodiversity is judged on repeatability, not on one good survey. Fixed
plots, consistent timing, and honest counts are what let you show change
across years. The key principle: if you change the method, the numbers
stop being comparable, so change the method only deliberately and record
it.

## Prerequisites

- A clear question, such as which habitat is losing species fastest
- Permission to enter the land, and a local guide to its species
- A written survey protocol with fixed dates and plot codes

## Materials & Tools

- A measuring tape or a fixed transect line marked at intervals
- A quadrat frame of 0.5 m by 0.5 m or 1 m by 1 m
- A clipboard and waterproof data forms
- A camera with a date stamp for repeatable photographs
- A species identification guide for the region
- A hand lens and a 10x loupe
- A GPS receiver, or a phone with offline maps
- A compass, a slope meter, and a mallet and posts for marking plots
- A brush for cleaning the quadrat between samples

## Procedure

### Design the Survey

1. Choose one indicator species group and one habitat to survey first, and
   decide the sample size before starting; 20 to 30 plots is a workable
   minimum.
2. Mark permanent plots with a post and a unique code, and record the
   coordinates, elevation, slope, and aspect of every plot.
3. Set a survey window so that all plots are sampled in the same two weeks
   each year.
4. Decide who counts what, so two observers record the same things.

### Count at Each Plot

1. Arrive at the plot and record the date, the time, and the observer,
   then walk the transect or place the quadrat at the marked intervals.
2. Count each plant species in the quadrat, or estimate percent cover.
3. Count animals by sight, by sound, or with a net, within a fixed time of
   effort.
4. Photograph the plot from the same fixed points every visit, note
   disturbance, grazing, fire damage, and illegal activity, and clean the
   quadrat before moving on.

### Analyse and Report

1. Sum the counts for each species across all plots.
2. Calculate the Shannon index, H equals minus the sum of p times the
   natural log of p, where p is each species' share of the total.
3. Calculate the Simpson index as one minus the sum of the squared shares,
   and compare both indices against the previous year.
4. Report the raw counts beside the indices, and record every change of
   method as a break in the series.

## Safety Notes

- Tell someone your route, the plot codes, and your expected return time.
- Carry a charged phone, a paper map, and a compass, because maps fail
  without signal.
- Wear closed shoes in long grass and watch for snakes, scorpions, and
  stinging plants.
- Stay off unstable ground and away from slips and bogs during and after
  rain.
- Do not remove, disturb, or collect the organisms you are counting.
- Keep off private, fenced, mining, and military land without permission.
