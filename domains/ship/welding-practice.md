# Welding Practice and Inspection

> **Domain:** ship | **Difficulty:** journeyman | **Safety:** red

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Welding is what turns loose plate into a structure, and the quality of the
joint is the quality of the vessel. The key principle is that welding is a
controlled process and not a craft guess: a qualified procedure, a
qualified welder, the right preheat, and an inspection that is written
down and kept. Almost every defect that matters in service was cheap to
prevent and expensive to find later.

## Prerequisites

- The structural design and the weld symbol convention
- The material grades and their carbon equivalents
- Hot work safety, fire watch, and the permit system
- Reading a welding procedure specification and a welder's qualification
- Confined space entry and gas testing

## Materials & Tools

- Welding procedure specifications and the welder's qualification records
- A constant current set for manual arc work, or a MIG set with shielding
  gas and a fume extractor
- E6013 electrodes for general work and E7018 low-hydrogen electrodes for
  the critical joints
- An electrode holder, a grinder, a chipping hammer, and a wire brush
- Gauges, a straightedge, and a fillet weld gauge
- A preheat torch and temperature crayons or a contact thermometer
- A dye penetrant kit and a magnetic particle kit
- A calibrated ultrasonic set for the thick butt welds

## Procedure

### Write the procedure

1. Choose the process: manual arc for site work and repair, MIG for
   production on mild steel, flux cored for out-of-position work, and
   submerged arc for the long shop seams.
2. Specify the joint: for a butt joint prepare a bevel of 30 to 45
   degrees, a root face of 1 to 3 mm, and a root gap of 1 to 3 mm.
3. Specify the parameters: the current and voltage range and the travel
   speed that gives a heat input of 0.5 to 2.5 kJ/mm for most fillet
   welds, and the maximum interpass temperature.
4. Specify the preheat: for carbon steel with a carbon equivalent above
   0.45 or a thickness above 40 mm, preheat to 100 to 150 degrees and
   hold until the whole area has reached it.

### Weld the joint

1. Verify the fit-up: the mismatch within 1 to 2 mm, the root gap
   correct, and the surface clean of oil, paint, and loose scale.
2. Set the machine and run a test weld on the same plate, checking the
   profile, the penetration, and the slag.
3. Tack with the same consumable, keep the tacks small, and clean each
   tack before welding over it.
4. Weld the root, then the fill passes, then the cap, each pass inside
   the interpass limit, using a back-step sequence on a long seam to
   spread the heat.
5. Clean the slag between passes and inspect each pass as it is made.

### Control the distortion

1. Follow a sequence that puts the most shrinkage where it does the least
   harm: butt the seams, then fill, then cap, and work from the fixed
   point outward.
2. Use the balanced method on long seams: alternate the sides and step
   back along the seam.
3. Keep the tacks small and spaced; a heavy tack is a permanent distortion.
4. Measure the flange and the offsets after welding, and correct cold
   where the distortion is under about 1 percent.

### Inspect and record

1. Inspect visually to the ISO 5817 quality level B or C: no cracks, no
   slag, undercut no deeper than 0.5 mm for level B, and the profile
   within the stated limits.
2. Check the fillet leg size to within plus or minus 1 mm or 10 percent,
   whichever is the larger.
3. Carry out the non-destructive tests the design calls for: magnetic
   particle for ferromagnetic welds, dye penetrant for the others, and
   ultrasonic for the thick butt welds.
4. Record the result against the joint: the welder's stamp, the procedure
   number, the date, and the test report.

## Safety Notes

- Welding fume causes metal fume fever and permanent lung damage: extract
  at the source and use a respirable filter, not a simple dust mask.
- The arc throws intense infrared and ultraviolet light: use a helmet with
  the correct shade filter, and screen the area so that nobody nearby is
  burned.
- Hot metal, spatter, and slag cause burns and eye injuries: leather
  gloves, sleeves, and a face shield for grinding and chipping.
- Fire is the largest risk: a hot work permit, a fire watch on the vessel
  and in the space, control of the cylinders, and an extinguisher to hand.
- The arc is an electrical shock hazard on a ship's steel deck: use
  insulated gloves, keep the work dry, and never weld in the rain with
  the machine earthed to the hull.
- Never weld in a tank or a double bottom without a permit, ventilation,
  and a gas test for oxygen, flammable gas, and hydrogen.
- Strip galvanizing and any lead-containing coating before welding; the
  fume from it will poison the welder.
- Protect the people below: hot slag falls into the bilge and into the
  double bottom, where nobody is watching for it.
