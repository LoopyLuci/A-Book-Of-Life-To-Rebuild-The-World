# Polymer Chemistry and Resins

> **Domain:** chemistry | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources

## Overview

Almost every modern material is a polymer: the resin in a tool, the glue
in a joint, the fibre in a rope. Understanding whether a resin grows by
chain-growth or step-growth tells you how it cures, how strong it ends up,
and how much of it you can hold in a single batch. The key principle is
that long chains only form when enough small molecules actually react with
each other.

## Prerequisites

- Organic Functional Groups

## Materials & Tools

- Resin and hardener or catalyst components matched to the system
- Mixing cups, sticks, a graduated cylinder, and a scale
- Silicone or metal moulds, with release agent for repeatable parts
- A heat-resistant mixing mat and an oven or heated curing box
- Nitrile gloves, splash goggles, and a fume hood or forced ventilation
- A calibrated thermometer and a timer for cure schedules

## Procedure

### Stage 1: Classify the Reaction

1. Chain-growth reactions join monomers across double bonds one at a time.
2. Step-growth reactions join any two molecules that can react.
3. Chain-growth needs an initiator; step-growth needs accurate ratios.
4. Chain-growth gives high molecular weight early; step-growth needs high
   conversion.
5. Record which type you are working with before you mix anything.

### Stage 2: Estimate Molecular Weight

1. Use the Carothers relation, DP = 1 / (1 - p), where p is conversion.
2. At 50 percent conversion the number-average DP is only 2.
3. At 90 percent conversion DP is 10.
4. At 99 percent conversion DP is 100, and that is where real polymers
   begin.
5. Multiply DP by the average repeat unit mass for the molar mass.

### Stage 3: Mix and Cure a Resin

1. Measure every component by mass or volume as the data sheet states.
2. Mix slowly for two to three minutes, scraping the sides and base.
3. Pour into the mould while the mix is still flowing.
4. Cure to the data sheet schedule, commonly 24 hours at 20 to 25 °C.
5. Post-cure in an oven, often 2 hours at 60 °C, when specified.

### Stage 4: Control the Batch and the Exotherm

1. Keep batches under 100 g until you know the cure behaviour.
2. Watch the centre of the mass, which runs hotter than the edges.
3. A large epoxy batch can reach 120 to 150 °C in the core.
4. Never seal a hot mixed resin in a closed container.
5. Cool slowly to keep the part flat and free of internal stress.

## Safety Notes

- Uncured epoxy and polyester resin are skin sensitisers; wear nitrile
  gloves and change them often.
- Styrene and vinyl monomer are volatile and flammable; work with
  ventilation and away from any ignition source.
- Methyl ethyl ketone peroxide is a strong oxidiser and can ignite
  accelerators; store it apart from resin and use only the amount stated.
- The curing reaction releases heat and can burn through thin mould walls;
  use a heat-resistant mat.
- Mixing resin is a hazardous operation and needs appropriate training,
  supervision, and equipment, including gloves, goggles, and ventilation.
- Dispose of uncured resin as hazardous waste, not as rubbish.
