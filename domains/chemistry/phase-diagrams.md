# Phase Diagrams and Phase Transitions

> **Domain:** chemistry | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** green

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

A phase diagram maps which phase is stable as temperature and pressure change,
and every line on it is a measured equilibrium. Reading them explains why ice
floats, how drying agents work, and where a salt solution stops freezing. The
key principle is that crossing a line changes phase, and the Clapeyron
relation fixes the slope of that line from the heat and volume change.

## Prerequisites

- Comfort with temperature, pressure, and their units
- A thermometer and a stated local atmospheric pressure
- Graph paper or a plotting tool for drawing phase lines

## Materials & Tools

- Thermometer covering -20 to 150 °C
- Barometer, or the published atmospheric pressure for your site
- Sealed glass ampoules of pure substances for melting-point checks
- Heating block, sand bath, or oil bath with stirring
- Ice, salt, and a warm water bath
- Notebook for plotting points and freezing curves
- Pressure vessel, only with appropriate training, supervision, and equipment

## Procedure

### Stage 1: Read the axes

1. Draw temperature on the horizontal axis and pressure on the vertical axis,
   usually in pascals or atmospheres.
2. Mark the triple point, where the solid, liquid, and gas lines meet.
3. Mark the critical point, beyond which liquid and gas are the same phase.

### Stage 2: Trace the solid-liquid line

1. Follow the fusion curve up from the triple point; its lean gives the sign
   of the volume change on melting.
2. Use the Clapeyron equation dP/dT = ΔH / (T · ΔV) to predict how the melting
   point moves as pressure changes.
3. Expect the line for water to lean the other way, so pressure lowers the
   melting point of ice.

### Stage 3: Apply the lever rule

1. Locate the overall composition on the horizontal axis of a binary diagram.
2. Draw a tie line across to the two-phase boundaries and measure the two
   segments it makes.
3. Divide each segment by the whole to get the mass fraction of each phase in
   the mixture.

### Stage 4: Use the diagram in practice

1. Predict the freezing behaviour of a salt solution from the liquidus curve;
   the more solute there is, the wider the freezing depression.
2. Choose a drying agent by picking the substance whose vapour pressure
   stays low across the working temperature range.
3. Plan a freeze-drying cycle by following the solid-gas line and holding
   the pressure below the triple-point pressure.

## Safety Notes

- Pressurised vessels are dangerous and pressurised glass can shatter. Work
  with them only with appropriate training, supervision, and equipment.
- Sealed ampoules under heat can burst, so heat them slowly and leave head
  space above the liquid.
- Never handle liquid nitrogen or dry ice bare-handed. Use a face shield and
  cryogenic gloves, and work with ventilation because it displaces oxygen.
- Hot oil baths reach 200 °C and ignite if splashed onto a flame. Use the
  lowest temperature that does the job and keep a lid nearby.
- Never pressure-test an improvised vessel. Use equipment rated for the
  pressure and the temperature.
- Cryogenic burns look like burns but damage tissue slowly. Rewarm slowly
  with lukewarm water, never with direct heat.
