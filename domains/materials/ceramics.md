# Ceramics

> **Domain:** materials | **Difficulty:** journeyman | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Ceramics turn clay into containers, tiles, filters, and refractory parts that
survive heat and moisture. The craft is mostly control: consistent clay, slow
drying, and a firing curve matched to the body and glaze. Most failures come
from wet or uneven pieces, soluble salts, or a glaze that does not fit the clay.

## Prerequisites

- Clay source and water
- Kiln or pit-firing experience
- Safe handling of dust and heat

## Materials & Tools

- Clay body and temper (sand, grog, or crushed fired clay)
- Water and a clean mixing surface
- Plaster bats or boards for drying
- Kiln shelves, posts, and cones
- Lead-free glaze materials
- Dust mask, eye protection, and heat-resistant gloves
- Buckets, sieve, and scales

## Procedure

### 1. Test the clay

1. Dig or buy clay and remove stones and roots.
2. Make small test tiles and measure drying and firing shrinkage.
3. Add temper if the clay cracks or is too plastic; mix thoroughly and age it
   for several days.
4. Fire a test tile to the intended temperature before committing a batch.

### 2. Form and dry

1. Wedge the clay to remove air pockets and even out moisture.
2. Form by pinching, coiling, slabs, or a wheel; keep joins damp and score them.
3. Dry slowly under cloth or plastic, away from sun and drafts, until leather
   hard and then bone dry.
4. Do not fire a piece that still feels cool or heavy with moisture.

### 3. Bisque fire

1. Load shelves with adequate spacing and support each piece evenly.
2. Fire slowly through the water-smoking stage, then raise to the bisque
   temperature recommended for the clay.
3. Cool the kiln slowly; rapid cooling can crack ware.
4. Inspect for cracks and sand rough edges before glazing.

### 4. Glaze and fire

1. Use lead-free glazes for anything that will touch food.
2. Apply an even coat, keep glaze off kiln shelves, and clean the foot ring.
3. Fire to the glaze maturity temperature with cones visible through the peep
   hole.
4. Test finished ware for crazing, leaching, and thermal shock before daily use.

## Safety Notes

- **Yellow hazard:** Clay and glaze dust contain crystalline silica. Mix wet,
  wear a respirator, and never dry-sweep the work area.
- Kilns reach over 1,000 C and can cause severe burns; use gloves, tongs, and a
  clear cool-down period.
- Fuel kilns produce carbon monoxide. Fire outdoors or in a ventilated space
  with a carbon monoxide alarm.
- Lead glazes are a serious poisoning risk, especially for children. Use
  lead-free recipes and test food-contact surfaces.
