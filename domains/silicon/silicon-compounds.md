# Silicon Compounds, Silicates and Silicones

> **Domain:** silicon | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

Silicon forms more compounds than any other element, and most of them fall
into three families: the silicates, which build glass, cement, and
ceramics; the silicones, which are polymers with an inorganic Si-O-Si
backbone; and the small-molecule compounds such as silicon carbide and
silane. The key principle is that the Si-O-Si bond is both strong and
flexible, so a network of linked tetrahedra can be melted into glass or
sintered into rock without breaking the chemistry.

## Prerequisites

- Reading a Mohs hardness chart and a simple mineral identification key
- Basic furnace and kiln operation with temperature control
- Casting and moulding practice for ceramics and polymers
- Handling of the acids and alkalis used in the glass and cement batch

## Materials & Tools

- Silica: quartz sand, flint, and diatomaceous earth
- Fluxes and stabilisers: soda ash, lime, feldspar, clay, and gypsum
- Silicone oil, grease, PDMS rubber, resin, and sealant
- Silicon carbide grit, refractory bricks, and crucibles
- Kiln and furnace with a pyrometer, moulds, and casting frames
- Diamond or carbide tooling, and a bench lathe for accurate parts
- Sieves, a mortar or ball mill for mixing, and a drying oven

## Procedure

### Identify and prepare the mineral

1. Recognise the silicate families by how the SiO4 tetrahedra are linked:
   isolated in quartz, paired in amphibole, in sheets in mica, and as a
   framework in feldspar.
2. Confirm quartz by its Mohs hardness of 7, its conchoidal fracture, and
   its resistance to acid.
3. Choose the mineral for the job: quartz for glass, clay for ceramics and
   cement, mica for electrical insulation.
4. Wash, dry, and sieve the batch to remove the iron and the clay that
   would discolour the melt.

### Melt glass and fire ceramics

1. Batch the raw materials at about 70-75% silica, 10-15% soda ash, and
   5-10% lime, with a small percentage of cullet.
2. Melt at 1400-1600 °C, then clear the batch, allowing about 10% loss to
   volatilisation, and let it settle for 6 to 12 hours.
3. Work the melt on a marver or blow it, then anneal the finished piece
   slowly from 500 °C down to 200 °C to relieve the internal stress.
4. Fire cement clinker at about 1450 °C and grind it with gypsum to make
   Portland cement; fire refractory bricks from magnesium silicate at about
   1500 °C.

### Work with silicones and silicon carbide

1. Cast PDMS from a 10:1 base to curing agent mix; the pot life is about
   two hours at 25 °C and the full cure takes 24 to 48 hours.
2. Cure at 60-100 °C for 1 to 4 hours wherever the part tolerance allows
    it.
3. Cut and grind silicone with carbide or diamond tooling at low speed
    with plenty of coolant, because the material is soft and gums the
    tool.
4. Grind or cut silicon carbide with diamond wheels or a water jet, and
    fire silicon carbide elements to about 1600 °C, above which the
    element oxidises.

## Safety Notes

- Molten glass and furnace slag cause severe burns; use dry gloves, a face
  shield, and a dry, clutter-free work area.
- Silica dust causes silicosis; wet-clean and never dry-sweep or sandblast
  silica, and wear a respirator when grinding.
- Hydrofluoric acid attacks silicate glass; never clean glass with it, and
  use a mild detergent instead.
- Uncured silicone releases vapours that irritate the air passages;
  ventilate the workspace and cure the part fully before use.
- Silane ignites spontaneously in air and is toxic; handle it only in a
  facility with a gas cabinet and a scrubber.
- Kilns above 500 °C need a cool-down period and a lid that does not seal
  the chamber while the kiln is hot.
