# Cleanroom Protocols and Contamination Control

> **Domain:** silicon | **Difficulty:** apprentice | **Safety:** yellow

*Reference material only. Verify this information against primary sources
and qualified professionals before acting on it. This entry is not a sole
source of information.*

## Overview

A cleanroom exists to keep particles and molecules off the wafer, because a
single 1 um particle landing on a 0.5 um feature destroys the device. The
key principle is that contamination has three sources, the people, the
surfaces, and the tools, and each needs its own control, so gowning,
airflow, tool protocols, and waste handling form one chain rather than four
separate habits. Cleanliness is a measured property, and the particle counts
are the evidence.

## Prerequisites

- How the tools contaminate a wafer: wear, outgassing, and particles from
  moving parts
- Hazard communication for the chemicals used at the wet bench
- Gowning technique and safe movement in a controlled space
- Access control and record keeping for a shared facility

## Materials & Tools

- Cleanroom with HEPA-filtered unidirectional airflow and a pressure
  cascade
- Garments: hair cover, hood, coverall, booties, face mask, goggles, and
  nitrile gloves
- Particle counter for 0.3 and 0.5 um counts, cleanroom wipes, and tacky
  mats
- Wet bench with separate acid and solvent stations, PTFE and polyethylene
  bottles, and a fume hood
- Labelled waste containers in secondary containment, a spill kit, and a
  first aid box with calcium gluconate gel
- Wafer carriers, transfer pods, and a dedicated tool set per station

## Procedure

### Classify and dress

1. Learn the classification: ISO 5 (Class 100) at the tool, ISO 7 (Class
   1000) in the corridor, and ISO 8 (Class 10000) in the grey space.
2. Gown in order, hair cover, hood, coverall, booties, then gloves, and
   never re-touch an outer surface.
3. Move deliberately and walk slowly, because a quick step releases a burst
   of particles from the garment.
4. Keep the bay uncrowded and work one person per bench, since two people
   at one station undo each other's discipline.

### Control the air and the tools

1. Check the differential pressure at the start of the shift, typically
   0.01 to 0.02 inches of water gauge from the corridor into the cleanroom.
2. Count the particles and log the readings at the same place and the same
   time each shift, so that a drift becomes visible.
3. Bring every open chamber, carousel, or hood into the bay; never open one
   in the corridor.
4. Schedule the wet cleaning and the heavy maintenance outside production
   hours, and re-count the room before returning it to service.

### Run the wet bench and the waste

1. Separate the acid station from the solvent station and keep their tools
   apart; the two never share a hose, a bottle, or a drain.
2. Rinse the tools in the approved order: deionized water first, then the
    process chemical.
3. Keep hydrofluoric acid in polyethylene or PTFE and never in glass, and
    label every bottle with the chemical, the concentration, and the date.
4. Segregate the waste into acid, base, solvent, heavy metal, and general
    aqueous streams, keep the containers under three-quarters full and in
    secondary containment, and never pour anything down the drain.

## Safety Notes

- Hydrofluoric acid causes deep burns and can kill through absorption; it
  needs trained handling, calcium gluconate gel at hand, and a buddy
  system.
- Piranha mixes sulfuric acid with hydrogen peroxide; it reacts violently
  with organics and reaches over 100 °C, so never seal it and never mix it
  with acetone or alcohol.
- Solvents are flammable: no open flame, bond and ground the containers,
  and use an explosion-proof ventilated enclosure.
- Gowning limits your speed and mobility in an emergency; keep the aisles
  clear and know the exit and the eyewash location.
- Never eat, drink, or pipette by mouth in the cleanroom.
- Ozone and perchloric clean recipes are hazardous and belong to trained
  operators only.
